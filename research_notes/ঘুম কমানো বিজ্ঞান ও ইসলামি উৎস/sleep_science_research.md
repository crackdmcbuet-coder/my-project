Evidence-strength tags used below: [STRONG] = consensus statement, large meta-analysis, or replicated controlled lab work; [MODERATE] = single well-controlled study or several smaller consistent ones; [WEAK] = small, observational, anecdotal, historical, or secondary-source only; [UNVERIFIED] = from memory or secondary source that I could not check against the primary paper in this session.

## 1. How much sleep do adults need, and what does chronic short sleep (and long sleep) cost?

### Takeaway
Both major 2015 consensus statements say healthy adults need 7+ hours (AASM/SRS) or 7–9 hours (NSF). Controlled lab work shows that 6 h/night for two weeks degrades performance to the level of 1 night of total sleep deprivation (and 4 h/night to the level of 2 nights). People largely do not notice this decline. Population meta-analyses link both short sleep (<6–7 h) and long sleep (>8–9 h) to higher mortality. The long-sleep link is probably driven largely by confounders such as depression, sleep apnea and underlying illness.

### Cited Findings

**Consensus statements [STRONG]**
- AASM + Sleep Research Society joint consensus (Watson et al., 2015): "Adults should sleep 7 or more hours per night on a regular basis to promote optimal health." Panel of 15 experts, modified RAND Appropriateness Method, target ages 18–60. Sleeping ≤6 h was judged inadequate to sustain health and safety. No upper limit was set: >9 h "may be appropriate" for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt and people who are ill. Published in SLEEP 38(6):843–844 (summary) and in J Clin Sleep Med 11(8):931–952 / SLEEP 38(8):1161–1183 (methodology and evidence). — [AASM consensus PDF](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf); [AASM press release via PR Newswire](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/seven-or-more-hours-of-sleep-per-night-a-health-necessity-for-adults-300091550.html)
- National Sleep Foundation (Hirshkowitz et al., 2015, *Sleep Health* 1:40–43): 18-member panel using the RAND/UCLA method. Recommended: young adults (18–25) and adults (26–64) 7–9 h; older adults (65+) 7–8 h; teenagers 8–10 h. Ranges are labelled "recommended," "may be appropriate for some individuals," or "not recommended." The guidelines apply to healthy people without sleep disorders. The panel notes that habitually sleeping outside the normal range may signal serious health problems. — [WashU profile of the NSF paper](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/national-sleep-foundations-sleep-time-duration-recommendations-me/); [EurekAlert news release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/554948)
  - [UNVERIFIED, from memory; the journal page returned 403] NSF "may be appropriate" bands: 18–25 y: 6 h or 10–11 h; 26–64 y: 6 h or 10 h; 65+ y: 5–6 h or 9 h. "Not recommended": <6 h for adults under 65, <5 h for 65+.

**Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003), SLEEP 26(2):117–126, "The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness" [STRONG, landmark lab study; verified from the full-text PDF]**
- Design: n = 48 healthy adults aged 21–38, randomized to 4, 6 or 8 h time in bed for 14 consecutive days, or 0 h for 3 days. Continuous lab monitoring. — [Full text PDF (gwern mirror)](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf); [PubMed 12683469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/)
- Restriction to 4 h or 6 h produced "significant cumulative, dose-dependent deficits in cognitive performance on all tasks." — [same PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf)
- After 14 days at 6 h, lapses in behavioral alertness and working memory reached the levels seen after **1 night of total sleep loss**. At 4 h they reached the levels of **2 nights without sleep**. The abstract's conclusion: "chronic restriction of sleep to 6 h or less per night produced cognitive performance deficits equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation." — [same PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf)
- Subjective sleepiness rose acutely and then nearly plateaued. It "did not significantly differentiate the 6 h and 4 h conditions," and "subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits." — [same PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf)
- Model: lapses rose near-linearly with cumulative wakefulness beyond **15.84 h/day (s.e. 0.73)**. This implies an average daily sleep need of **8.16 ± 0.73 h** to prevent accumulating deficits in this sample. — [same PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf)
- People who habitually slept longest tended to be more affected by restriction (r = 0.29, P = 0.048). — [same PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf)

**Belenky et al. (2003), J Sleep Res 12:1–12 (Walter Reed Army Institute) [STRONG]**
- Design: 3, 5, 7 or 9 h time in bed for 7 days (n = 18/16/16/16), then 3 recovery nights of 8 h. — [PubMed 12603781](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/); [PDF copy](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios601/Surveys/Belenky2003.pdf)
- The 3-h group declined steadily. The 5-h and 7-h groups declined and then stabilized at a reduced level. After 3 recovery nights, the 5-h and 7-h groups did **not** return to baseline, and the 3-h group recovered only partly. The 9-h group stayed at baseline. The authors concluded that the brain "adapts" to chronic restriction by settling at a lower level of performance, not by restoring normal performance. — [PubMed 12603781](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/)

**Mortality meta-analyses (observational) [STRONG for association, WEAK for causation]**
- Cappuccio et al. (2010), SLEEP 33(5):585–592: 16 prospective studies, 1,382,999 participants, 112,566 deaths, 4–25 years of follow-up. Short sleep RR **1.12** (95% CI 1.06–1.18). Long sleep RR **1.30** (95% CI 1.22–1.38). "Both short and long duration of sleep are significant predictors of death." — [PMC2864873](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864873/); [Warwick repository](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/5980/)
- Jike et al. (2018), Sleep Med Rev 39:25–36, on long sleep only: 137 prospective cohorts, 5,134,036 participants. Long sleep was associated with mortality RR **1.39** (1.31–1.47), CVD 1.25, stroke 1.46 and CHD 1.24. Meta-regression showed a linear dose-response. — [PubMed 28890167](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28890167/); [Kyoto repository full text](https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/server/api/core/bitstreams/3ef22e57-e5f0-4c96-95fa-1b24762d9a4b/content)
- Grandner & Drummond (2007), Sleep Med Rev 11(5):341–360, "Who are the long sleepers?": epidemiology repeatedly links long sleep to mortality without explaining why. Candidate explanations include sleep fragmentation, fatigue, immune function, photoperiod abnormalities, "lack of challenge," **depression**, and **underlying disease**. Depression, **sleep apnea**, heart disease and general failing health may mediate or confound the link. — [PMC3755488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3755488); [Arizona experts page](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/who-are-the-long-sleepers-towards-an-understanding-of-the-mortali/)

### Inferences
- For a healthy adult, the best-supported target is about 7–9 h of actual sleep. Going below about 6 h regularly carries measurable cognitive costs that the sleeper typically underestimates. That is the central caution for anyone trying to "train" themselves to sleep less.
- For someone who sleeps >9–10 h and still feels unrefreshed, the long-sleep literature suggests looking for a cause (depression, sleep apnea, thyroid, anemia, medication, poor-quality sleep). The answer is unlikely to be simply cutting sleep. Long sleep may be a marker of illness rather than its cause.
- A reasonable approach for an oversleeper is to bring sleep down into the 7–9 h band, not below 7 h. This fits both consensus statements.

### Gaps
- Exact NSF "may be appropriate" numbers could not be verified (journal page returned 403). They are flagged above.
- Kripke et al. (2002), Arch Gen Psychiatry, the 1.1-million-person ACS study with lowest mortality at about 7 h, was not checked in this session.
- Whether long sleep *causes* harm (rather than signalling it) is unresolved. No RCT exists, and Mendelian-randomization work was not reviewed here.

## 2. Familial natural short sleepers (FNSS): genes, rarity, and whether others can train themselves to need less sleep

### Takeaway
A few families carry rare mutations (DEC2/BHLHE41, ADRB1, NPSR1, GRM1, SIK3) that let them feel rested on roughly 4–6.5 h with no apparent harm. These mutations are extremely rare: NPSR1-Y206H is estimated at about 1 in 4 million. No controlled evidence shows that non-carriers can train themselves into a lower sleep need. Lab data show instead that self-selected short sleepers carry sleep debt and that people adapt only at a lower performance level.

### Cited Findings
- **DEC2 (BHLHE41), He et al. 2009, *Science* 325(5942):866–870** (Fu & Ptáček lab, UCSF): first human short-sleep gene. The P385R mutation was found in 2 family members who averaged **6.25 h/night vs 8.06 h** for non-carrier relatives. Mice with P385R slept less, and flies showed reduced sleep. DEC2 knockout mice slept only slightly more, suggesting a gain-of-function effect. — [PMC2884988](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884988); [UCSF 2009 news](https://www.ucsf.edu/news/2009/08/4281/first-human-gene-implicated-regulating-length-human-sleep); [Davidson genomics summary](https://www.bio.davidson.edu/genomics/2012/Ford/CharlieShorSleeperPage2.html) [MODERATE: tiny n, robust animal model]
- **ADRB1, Shi et al. 2019, *Neuron*** (published 28 Aug 2019): the A187V variant in the β1-adrenergic receptor, found in a FNSS family. Knock-in mice slept **about 55 min less** (NREM −53 min, REM −7 min). The mutant receptor is less stable, and ADRB1 neurons in the dorsal pons are wake-promoting. — [PMC6763376](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6763376); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20190901/Discovery-of-second-gene-responsible-natural-short-sleep.aspx)
- **NPSR1, Xing et al. 2019, *Science Translational Medicine* 11(514)**: the NPSR1-Y206H mutation, found in a father and son who sleep about 5.5 h and 4.3 h. Mutant mice slept less and resisted sleep-deprivation memory deficits. Reported rarity: about **1 in 4 million** people. — [UCSF news Oct 2019](https://ucsf.edu/news/2019/10/415706/new-gene-helps-explain-why-some-people-need-less-sleep); [PubMed 31619542](https://www.pubmed.gov/31619542/); [Discover Magazine](https://www.discovermagazine.com/this-gene-helps-explain-why-some-people-can-get-by-on-little-sleep-40308)
- **GRM1, Shi et al., *Current Biology* (online Oct 2020; 2021 issue; DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2020.09.071)**: two GRM1 mutations in two unrelated families. Mutant mice slept less (about 30 min/day per a secondary summary) without obvious health problems. — [Knowable Magazine 2024](https://knowablemagazine.org/content/article/mind/2024/genetics-of-people-who-need-little-sleep); [The Paper (Chinese summary)](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_17213284) [mouse figure from a secondary source only]
- **SIK3-N783Y (Chen, Xing et al., PNAS 2025, Ptáček & Fu co-authors)**: a fifth gene linked to the natural short sleep trait. — [CARTA entry](https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/331778); [National Geographic overview](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/health/article/natural-short-sleepers-genetics-sleep-need) [details not verified against the paper]
- Definition used by the group: a lifelong tendency to wake naturally after **4–6 h** of sleep feeling restored. — [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/health/article/natural-short-sleepers-genetics-sleep-need)
- A 2024 NIH grant abstract from the same lab states that the four known genes (DEC2, ADRB1, NPSR1, GRM1) explain only a minority of the roughly 30 FNSS families sequenced. — [NIH grant summary](https://api.ai-analytics.org/grant/nih/10893516.md) [secondary aggregator of an NIH abstract]
- No reliable population prevalence figure for FNSS was found. A "3% of Americans" claim circulates without a source. — [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-do-some-people-need-just-four-to-six-hours-of-sleep/)
- **Can non-carriers train themselves?** Klerman & Dijk (2005), *Sleep* 28(10):1253–1259: 17 healthy adults with habitual bed-rest of 6.1–10.3 h were given extended sleep opportunity. Those with **shorter** habitual sleep slept **more** when allowed. The authors concluded that short habitual sleepers carry higher sleep debt and that much individual variation reflects "self-selected sleep restriction" rather than lower need. — [PMC1351048](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1351048) [MODERATE]
- Van Dongen 2003 (Section 1): subjective sleepiness plateaus while objective deficits keep accumulating, so feeling adapted is not evidence of adaptation. — [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/melatonin/2003-vandongen.pdf)
- Belenky 2003 (Section 1): under 5–7 h, performance stabilizes at a lower level and does not recover after 3 nights of 8 h. — [PubMed 12603781](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/)
- Gradual sleep reduction studies (Mullaney, Johnson, Naitoh, Friedmann & Globus, *Psychophysiology* 14(3):237–244 and 245–250, 1977) exist. — [Scilit record 1](https://scilit.net/publications/366cced32bdff1f72094be9a428f4c67); [Scilit record 2](https://scilit.com/publications/d0fd670642f42eac1b62b70bf7682ea4)
  - [UNVERIFIED, from memory] Small samples of young couples reduced sleep over months toward about 4.5–6.5 h. Some reportedly still slept less than baseline at follow-up, but subjects reported fatigue at the lowest levels. Do not cite the specific numbers without checking the paper.

### Inferences
- If someone sleeps well over 9 h, it is realistic to aim at the 7–8 h range with good sleep hygiene and a regular schedule. Aiming at the 4–6 h of genetic short sleepers is not realistic for a person who lacks those mutations, and such carriers are extraordinarily rare.
- The only way to tell an FNSS carrier from a self-deprived short sleeper is lifelong history (since childhood), waking spontaneously without an alarm on free days, no weekend catch-up sleep, and no daytime sleepiness. Genetic confirmation is a research tool, not a clinical one.

### Gaps
- No RCT has tested long-term "sleep need training" in adults with objective outcomes.
- The Pellegrino et al. 2014 twin study (a second BHLHE41 variant, Y362H, conferring resistance to sleep deprivation) is [UNVERIFIED] and was not checked.
- Full results of the 1977 gradual-reduction studies were not retrieved.

## 3. Polyphasic and ultrashort sleep (Uberman/Everyman), Stampi's solo-sailor work, critiques, and split sleep research

### Takeaway
A 2021 NSF consensus panel found **no evidence of benefits** from polyphasic schedules. It judged schedules that cut total sleep or fragment it into many episodes to be associated with adverse physical, mental and performance outcomes, and it recommends against them. Stampi's ultrashort-sleep research was designed for emergencies (solo sailing, sustained military operations), where naps reduce the damage of unavoidable sleep loss but performance stays below fully rested levels. Splitting the *same* total sleep into a night block plus an afternoon nap (biphasic) can perform about as well as consolidated sleep for some cognitive measures. Cutting total sleep remains harmful.

### Cited Findings
- **Weaver et al. (2021), "Adverse impact of polyphasic sleep patterns in humans," *Sleep Health* (NSF consensus panel)**: screened 40,672 records, reviewed 2,023 in full text, retained 22. The panel found no evidence supporting benefits. It concluded that polyphasic schedules "and the sleep deficiency inherent in those schedules, are associated with a variety of adverse physical health, mental health, and performance outcomes." It also stated that "striving to adopt a schedule that significantly reduces the amount of sleep per 24 hours and/or fragments sleep into multiple episodes throughout the 24-hour day is not recommended." Naps and siestas were excluded from the definition of polyphasic sleep. — [PubMed 33795195](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33795195/); [Oxford NDCN listing](https://www.ndcn.ox.ac.uk/publications/1171137) [STRONG]
- **Claudio Stampi (ed.), *Why We Nap: Evolution, Chronobiology, and Functions of Polyphasic and Ultrashort Sleep*, Birkhäuser, Boston, 1992.** Stampi ran a Canadian-government-funded project, "Ultrashort sleep: a strategy for optimal performance during sustained operations" (University of Ottawa, 1987–1989), and studied solo ocean sailors (James Maas cited his observation of more than 100 solo sailors). One documented sailor split sleep into 6–7 naps per day, concentrated at night. — [Wikipedia: Why We Nap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_We_Nap); [Wikipedia: Claudio Stampi](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Claudio_Stampi); [book PDF (gwern)](https://www.gwern.net/doc/zeo/1992-stampi-whywenap.pdf) [WEAK–MODERATE: field and case studies]
- Researcher caution summarized on Wikipedia: performance achieved using ultrashort sleep to replace normal sleep "is always well below" that of fully rested people. Wikipedia also reports a 50-day polyphasic trial by Stampi with one subject, in which sleep-stage proportions were roughly preserved. This was not verified against the book. — [Wikipedia: Polyphasic sleep](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Bimodal_sleep)
- **Piotr Woźniak (SuperMemo), "Polyphasic Sleep: Facts and Myths" (Jan 2005, updated) and "Polyphasic Sleep: 5 Years Later" (2010)**: a widely read critique arguing that Uberman-type schedules conflict with the circadian and homeostatic regulation of sleep and amount to chronic sleep deprivation. — [SuperMemo blog](https://www.supermemo.com/en/blog/polyphasic); [2010 follow-up](https://www-v1.supermemo.com/archives1990-2015/articles/polyphasic2010) [expert opinion, not peer-reviewed]
- **Split (biphasic) sleep with the same total, adolescents (Duke-NUS, Chee lab):**
  - Cousins, Van Rijn, Ong & Chee (2019), *Sleep*: 118 adolescents aged 15–19 over 3 nights. 9 h vs 6.5 h consolidated vs 5 h vs 5 h night + 1.5 h afternoon nap (6.5 h total). On topographical memory, the split group (about 0.76 accuracy) matched the 9-h controls, while the 6.5-h consolidated and 5-h groups scored about 0.66. Split-group total sleep time was slightly lower because of two sleep latencies. Working memory (n-back) showed little difference. The authors caution that this "is not a substitute for adequate nighttime sleep." — [PMC6448285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6448285) [MODERATE]
  - Lo, Chee et al. (2019), *Sleep*: 5 nights of continuous 6.5 h (n = 29) vs 5 h night + 1.5 h nap (n = 29). The split group had fewer vigilance lapses, better working memory and executive function, and better mood, **but** a larger glucose rise after a glucose load. The authors still advise getting the recommended nocturnal sleep. — [Duke-NUS publication page](https://www.duke-nus.edu.sg/nbd/sleeplab/publication/publications/detail/index/differential-effects-of-split-and-continuous-sleep-on-vigilance-and-glucose-tolerance-in-sleep-restricted-adolescents); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/sleep-teen-cognition-10798/) [MODERATE]

### Inferences
- Uberman (6 × 20-min naps, about 2 h total) and Everyman (a core block plus naps, about 3–4.5 h total) schedules have no controlled evidence of safety or benefit. They are a form of chronic sleep restriction and should be described as such.
- A biphasic pattern (a night block plus a siesta, same total of 7–8 h) is a legitimate and evidence-compatible option. It redistributes sleep rather than reducing it.

### Gaps
- No RCT of Uberman or Everyman schedules in adults was found. Evidence is anecdotal or from small case studies.
- Adult split-sleep studies (e.g., Jackson et al. 2014; Kosmadopoulos et al. on split shifts) were not retrieved in this session. The split-sleep evidence above is in adolescents.

## 4. Segmented / biphasic night sleep (Wehr, Ekirch), critiques (Yetish), and siesta cultures

### Takeaway
Wehr's 1992 NIMH experiment showed that when people were given 14 h of darkness per night for weeks, their sleep tended to split into two blocks separated by 1–2 h of quiet wakefulness. Historian Roger Ekirch documented "first sleep" and "second sleep" in pre-industrial Europe. Actigraphy of modern hunter-gatherers (Yetish 2015) found consolidated sleep of 5.7–7.1 h with no regular "second sleep," little napping, and very low insomnia. So segmented sleep appears to be a possible response to long dark nights, not a universal "natural" pattern. None of this literature shows that total sleep can be reduced. Wehr's subjects actually slept *more*.

### Cited Findings
- **Wehr (1992), "In short photoperiods, human sleep is biphasic," *J Sleep Res* 1(2):103–107**: volunteers switched from a 16-h to a 10-h light period (14 h dark). Sleep lengthened and usually divided into two roughly symmetrical bouts of several hours, separated by a 1–3 h waking interval. Nocturnal melatonin secretion lengthened. — [PubMed 10607034](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10607034/); [Scientific American blog](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/a-blog-around-the-clock/what-is-a-natural-sleep-pattern/) [MODERATE: small lab study]
  - The Wikipedia summary says subjects averaged about 11 h on the first night (repaying sleep debt). By week 4 this fell to about 8 h, split into two blocks of about 3–5 h with 1–2 h of quiet wakefulness between them. — [Wikipedia: Thomas Wehr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wehr) [orientation only; subject number and exact durations not verified from the primary abstract]
- **A. Roger Ekirch, "Sleep We Have Lost: Pre-industrial Slumber in the British Isles," *American Historical Review* 106(2):343–386/387 (April 2001)**, expanded in ***At Day's Close: Night in Times Past* (W.W. Norton, 2005)**. Early-modern Europeans woke from a "first sleep" around midnight, stayed awake for up to about an hour, then had a "second" (or "morning") sleep until dawn. — [Ekirch sleep research page (Virginia Tech)](https://sites.google.com/vt.edu/roger-ekirch/sleep-research); [JSTOR Daily](https://daily.jstor.org/how-the-age-of-the-bed-changed-the-way-we-sleep) [historical evidence: WEAK–MODERATE as physiology]
- Critique: Niall Boyce (2023, *Medical History*), "Have we lost sleep? A reconsideration of segmented sleep in early modern England," argues the theory "deserves reconsideration." Ekirch replied in 2024 and admitted that the shift to consolidated sleep happened later and more unevenly than he had first proposed. — [Boyce 2023 PDF (Cambridge)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B70D0BFF8E77CFB81A839E9B72240CF2/S0025727323000145a.pdf/have-we-lost-sleep-a-reconsideration-of-segmented-sleep-in-early-modern-england.pdf); [Ekirch 2024 reply (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/85ACD24ADCEEA5725E3CADC63DB58922/S0025727324000206a.pdf/reflections-on-have-we-lost-sleep.pdf)
- **Yetish et al. (2015), "Natural sleep and its seasonal variations in three pre-industrial societies," *Current Biology* 25:2862–2868** (Hadza in Tanzania, San in Namibia, Tsimane in Bolivia; Siegel lab, UCLA); verified from the full text:
  - Sleep duration averaged **5.7–7.1 h**, and the sleep period (onset to offset) **6.9–8.5 h**. This is "near the low end of those industrial societies." The overall average was about 6.4 h, with about 1 h more in winter than in summer. — [Full text PDF (Gurven lab)](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf)
  - Sleep onset came on average **3.3 h after sunset**, and waking usually before sunrise. Sleep fell during the nightly fall in ambient temperature, which the authors propose as a major regulator. — [same](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf)
  - "No regular period of activity was seen in the night, consistent with the lack of a 'second sleep'." The bimodal pattern "may have been a consequence of longer winter nights in higher latitudes." — [same](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf)
  - Napping was rare. The San showed 0 afternoon naps in 210 winter days and naps on 3% of summer days. Counting all potential naps would add only about 7 min/day in summer. — [same](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf)
  - Insomnia: neither group had a word for it. Only 1.5% (onset problems) and 2.5% (maintenance problems) reported regular problems, versus 10–30% chronic insomnia in industrial societies. — [same](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf) [MODERATE: actigraphy, not EEG]
- Siesta cultures and napping health data (see also Section 5): meta-analyses link **long** daytime naps (≥60 min) with higher cardiovascular risk and mortality, while short naps show no significant association. Yamada et al. 2015 (*Sleep*, 11 cohorts) found naps ≥60 min associated with CVD rate ratio **1.82**, with a J-curve: risk did not rise up to about 30 min. Wang et al. 2024 (*PLOS ONE*, 21 cohorts) found naps ≥1 h associated with mortality HR **1.22**, while naps under 1 h showed no significant association. — [PMC4431364 (Yamada 2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4431364); [PMC11482734 (Wang 2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11482734/); [Liu 2015 Med Sci Monit](https://medscimonit.com/abstract/full/idArt/893186) [observational; confounding by illness likely]

### Inferences
- "Natural" human sleep, as measured in equatorial pre-industrial groups, is about 6–7 h of *measured* sleep within a 7–8.5 h sleep window. This is relevant for someone who currently spends 10+ h in bed: a 7–8 h window is closer to what is "natural."
- A night-waking interval (as in segmented sleep) is not a disorder if the person falls back asleep and feels rested. This fits worship practices that involve waking in the night, though the Islamic side is outside this note's scope.

### Gaps
- Primary Wehr 1992 abstract numbers (n, exact durations) were not retrieved, because PubMed blocked access.
- The Naska et al. 2007 (Arch Intern Med) Greek siesta cohort, reporting about 37% lower coronary mortality with regular siesta, was **not found/verified** in this session [UNVERIFIED].

## 5. Napping science: NASA, optimal nap length, sleep inertia, caffeine nap, Mednick

### Takeaway
Short naps of about 10–20 min (up to about 26 min) give quick alertness gains with little grogginess (sleep inertia). Naps of about 30 min or more cause sleep inertia for a while before benefits appear. Naps of 60–90 min that include slow-wave and REM sleep can consolidate learning. Caffeine taken just before a short nap ("caffeine nap") outperformed either alone in a driving-simulator study. Long habitual daytime naps (>60 min) are associated with worse health outcomes in observational data.

### Cited Findings
- **NASA cockpit nap: Rosekind, Graeber, Dinges, Connell et al. (1994), NASA Technical Memorandum 108839, "Crew Factors in Flight Operations IX"**: long-haul crews in a Rest group (n = 12) got a planned 40-min rest opportunity during cruise, while a No-Rest group (n = 9) did not. Pilots slept on 93% of opportunities, fell asleep in **5.6 min**, and slept **25.8 min**. The nap was associated with improved physiological alertness and performance through descent and landing. — [NASA NTRS 19950006379](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19950006379); [report PDF (ops.group copy)](https://ops.group/blog/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Crew-Factors-in-Flight-Operations-IX_-Effects-of-Planned-Cockpit-Rest-on-Crew-Performance-and-Alertness-in-Long-Haul-Operations.pdf) [MODERATE]
- The popular figures "**34% better performance, 54% better alertness**" are attributed to Rosekind et al. (1995), "Alertness management: strategic naps in operational settings," *J Sleep Res* 4(S2):62–66, according to the Sleep Foundation. The NTRS abstract does not contain these numbers. One analysis says 34% refers to reaction-time/vigilance (PVT) and 54% ("up to 54%") to EEG-based physiological alertness. The "26-minute ideal nap" is a popularization of the 25.8 min actually slept. — [Sleep Foundation: NASA nap](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-hygiene/nasa-nap); [SpaceDaily analysis](https://www.spacedaily.com/nasa-research-suggests-strategic-napping-improves-alertness-and-performance-and-most-workplaces-ignore-it/) [percentages not verified in primary text]
- **Brooks & Lack (2006), *Sleep* 29(6), "A brief afternoon nap following nocturnal sleep restriction: which nap duration is most recuperative?"**: after 5 h of nocturnal sleep, compared 0/5/10/20/30-min naps at about 3 pm (n = 24, crossover). The **10-min nap** was overall the most effective. The 30-min nap produced impaired alertness immediately afterwards (sleep inertia), followed by improvements lasting up to 155 min. The 5-min nap gave little benefit. — [PubMed 16796222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16796222/) [MODERATE]
- **Mednick, Nakayama & Stickgold (2003), "Sleep-dependent learning: a nap is as good as a night," *Nature Neuroscience* 6:697–698**: 60–90-min naps containing both slow-wave sleep and REM produced perceptual-learning (texture discrimination) gains similar to an 8-h night. The 90-min group improved significantly (8.4 ms, P = 0.008), while the 60-min group was marginal (P = 0.07). The finding is limited to one perceptual task. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1078); [PDF](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~knakayama/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/118natneuro_mednick_brief.pdf) [MODERATE]
- **Caffeine nap: Reyner & Horne (1997), *Psychophysiology* 34(6):721–725**: 12 sleepy drivers had a 30-min break with caffeine (200 mg) plus a <15-min nap before a 2-h monotonous afternoon simulator drive. Driving incidents fell to **9% of placebo levels**, versus 34% for caffeine alone (one abstract). An earlier 1996 study by the same authors used 150 mg caffeine with a <15-min nap and found that a break alone was ineffective. — [PubMed 9401427](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9401427/); [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/nl/publicatie/suppression-sleepiness-drivers-combination-caffiene-short-nap) [MODERATE: small n]
- Nap duration and health (observational): see Section 4 (Yamada 2015; Wang 2024). Naps ≥60 min were associated with higher CVD and mortality risk, and naps ≤30 min were not. — [PMC4431364](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4431364); [PMC11482734](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11482734/)
- Hunter-gatherers nap little (Yetish 2015, Section 4). — [PDF](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf)

### Inferences
- For someone reducing excessive nighttime sleep, a short early-afternoon nap of 10–20 min (around the Dhuhr–Asr period, i.e., a traditional *qaylula*) can cover the post-lunch dip without causing sleep inertia or eroding night sleep. Naps of 30–60 min risk grogginess, and long daily naps (>60 min) are a marker worth watching.
- A full 90-min cycle nap can be used occasionally after a short night, with inertia expected on waking.

### Gaps
- Sara Mednick's popular books (*Take a Nap! Change Your Life*, 2006; *The Power of the Downstate*, 2022) were not verified in this session [UNVERIFIED].
- Hayashi et al. (2003) on caffeine plus nap was not checked.

## 6. Sleep efficiency and quality: can you get the same benefit from less time in bed?

### Takeaway
Sleep restriction therapy (SRT, part of CBT-I) deliberately compresses time-in-bed to match actual sleep time. It raises sleep efficiency and consolidates sleep in people with insomnia, and it is efficacious on sleep-diary measures. It does **not** reduce the body's sleep need: it removes wasted, wakeful time in bed. Exercise, a warm bath 1–2 h before bed, avoiding caffeine for at least 6 h before bed, and avoiding alcohol all improve sleep quality modestly. A cool night-time temperature appears to be a natural regulator.

### Cited Findings
- **Sleep restriction therapy: Miller et al. (2014), *Sleep Medicine Reviews*, "The evidence base of sleep restriction therapy for treating insomnia disorder"** (Spielman co-author): 1,344 studies screened, but only 4 trials met standards. Standalone SRT "is efficacious for the treatment of chronic insomnia for sleep diary continuity variables," with moderate-to-large effect sizes for diary sleep efficiency. Evidence on objective measures was insufficient, and implementation varies. — [EM-Consulte abstract](https://em-consulte.com/article/921542/the-evidence-base-of-sleep-restriction-therapy-for) [MODERATE]
- Typical clinical protocol: if sleep efficiency (time asleep ÷ time in bed) is below about 85%, time in bed is reduced (e.g., by 15 min). At 85–90% the schedule is held, and above that, time in bed is extended. This is a clinical convention described in Sleep Foundation guidance. — [Sleep Foundation: sleep restriction therapy](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/insomnia/treatment/sleep-restriction-therapy) [convention; the original Spielman, Saskin & Thorpy 1987, *Sleep* 10:45–56, is UNVERIFIED in this session]
- **Exercise: Kredlow et al. (2015), *J Behav Med* 38(3):427–449** (66 studies): acute exercise had small benefits for total sleep time, sleep latency, efficiency and slow-wave sleep, and a moderate benefit for wake after sleep onset. Regular exercise had small benefits for total sleep time and efficiency, small-to-medium for latency, and moderate for subjective quality. — [PubMed 25596964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25596964/) [STRONG]
- **Warm bath or shower: Haghayegh et al. (2019), *Sleep Medicine Reviews*** (17 studies): water at about 40–43 °C (104–109 °F), taken 1–2 h before bed (optimum about 90 min), shortened sleep-onset latency by about 10 min on average and improved efficiency and quality. 15 min before bed was too late. — [UT Austin Cockrell School release](https://cockrell.utexas.edu/?p=23504); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/detroit/news/a-hot-bath-1-or-2-hours-before-bedtime-can-improve-your-sleep/) [MODERATE; numbers via press release]
- **Caffeine: Drake et al. (2013), *J Clin Sleep Med* 9(11), "Caffeine effects on sleep taken 0, 3, or 6 hours before going to bed"** (n = 12, 400 mg): caffeine even **6 h** before bed reduced objectively measured sleep by about **1 h**, and subjects did not notice. The authors suggest avoiding caffeine after about 5 pm. — [AASM press release](https://aasm.org/late-afternoon-and-early-evening-caffeine-can-disrupt-sleep-at-night/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/11/131114155838.htm) [MODERATE]
- **Alcohol: Ebrahim, Shapiro, Williams & Fenwick (2013), *Alcohol Clin Exp Res*, "Alcohol and sleep I: effects on normal sleep"** (about 20–27 lab studies): alcohol shortens sleep latency and increases deep sleep in the first half of the night, but disrupts the second half and reduces REM at moderate to high doses. The lead author: "Alcohol on the whole is not useful for improving a whole night's sleep." — [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/26489-alcohol-sleep.html); [Memory-Key summary](https://archive.memory-key.com/research/news/reviewing-alcohols-effects-normal-sleep) [STRONG review; details via secondary sources]
- **Temperature and light:** in Yetish 2015, sleep fell in the nightly period of falling ambient temperature. The authors propose temperature as a major regulator, "largely eliminated from modern sleep environments." Light exposure peaked in the morning. — [Yetish PDF](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf)
- **Circadian alignment:** Roenneberg (*Internal Time*, 2012) argues that "social jet lag," a mismatch between body clock and social schedule, drives chronic sleep deprivation, and recommends more daylight to sync the clock. — [The Marginalian review](https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/05/11/internal-time-till-roenneber/); [De Gruyter/Harvard UP](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/harvard.9780674065482/html)
- **Ramadan meal-timing evidence:** in Ramadan studies, a meal served 30 min before bed was associated with longer sleep latency. When dinner was served 3–3.5 h before bed, latency was unchanged. — [Almeneessier & BaHammam 2018 (PMC6292409)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6292409)

### Inferences
- The legitimate science-based way to "sleep less" is to **cut time in bed that isn't sleep**: keep a consistent wake time, go to bed only when sleepy, and gradually shrink time in bed until sleep efficiency is about 85–90%. The goal is not to cut sleep itself below about 7 h. This is the logic of SRT, and its evidence base is for insomnia, not for healthy people who want extra waking hours.
- Quality boosters (regular exercise, morning daylight, a cool dark room, no caffeine after mid-afternoon, no alcohol, a warm shower about 90 min before bed, no heavy meal right before bed) may make the same sleep more restorative. No evidence shows that they lower total sleep need by hours.
- [Inference] SRT can cause daytime sleepiness in its early weeks and is usually supervised clinically. Caution is needed for people with bipolar disorder, epilepsy or sleep apnea, or who drive. This is general clinical knowledge, not verified in this session.

### Gaps
- No study was found showing that increasing slow-wave sleep proportion lets healthy people reduce total sleep time safely. The claim that "deep sleep is the only sleep that matters" is not established. Horne's "core sleep" idea (Section 8) is the closest theoretical basis and is contested.
- The original Spielman 1987 paper was not retrieved.

## 7. Excessive sleepiness / oversleeping: causes and when to see a doctor

### Takeaway
Feeling a need for >9–10 h, or being sleepy despite long sleep, can reflect chronic insufficient or poor-quality sleep, obstructive sleep apnea, depression, medication effects, hypothyroidism, anemia or iron/B12 deficiency, circadian rhythm disorders, narcolepsy or idiopathic hypersomnia. Red flags that warrant medical evaluation (a sleep study, thyroid tests, blood count) include loud snoring or breathing pauses, sleepiness while driving, symptoms lasting ≥3 months, and sleepiness that disrupts work or relationships.

### Cited Findings
- Long sleep may be mediated or confounded by depression, sleep apnea, heart disease and failing health (Grandner & Drummond 2007). — [PMC3755488](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3755488)
- The AASM/SRS consensus advises that people "concerned that they are sleeping too little or too much should consult their healthcare provider." It also notes that >9 h may be appropriate for young adults, people recovering from sleep debt, or people who are ill. — [AASM consensus PDF](https://www.aasm.org/resources/pdf/adultsleepdurationconsensus.pdf)
- The NSF panel notes that habitually sleeping outside the recommended range may signal serious health problems. — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/554948)
- Hypersomnia differs from being a long sleeper: heavy sleepers feel refreshed after long sleep, while people with hypersomnia remain tired regardless. Listed causes include chronic insufficient sleep, sleep apnea, narcolepsy, medications, depression, irregular schedules, hypothyroidism, anemia, iron/B12/vitamin D deficiency, diabetes, and liver or kidney disease. Idiopathic hypersomnia is diagnosed when no cause is found, and symptoms must persist ≥3 months. — [Sleep Foundation: excessive sleepiness](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/sleep-disorders/excessive-sleepiness); [Sleep Foundation: hypersomnia tests and diagnosis](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/hypersomnia/tests-and-diagnosis); [Mayo Clinic Care Network definition](https://www.mymlc.com/health-information/definitions/h/hypersomnia/) [consumer-level sources; WEAK–MODERATE]
- See a doctor for excessive daytime sleepiness, loud snoring, observed pauses in breathing, or sleepiness while driving. Do not drive while sleepy until the cause is established. Evaluation may include a sleep diary, polysomnography or MSLT, and blood tests (thyroid etc.). — [Mayo Clinic Care Network](https://www.mymlc.com/health-information/definitions/h/hypersomnia/); [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/hypersomnia/tests-and-diagnosis)

### Inferences
- Before trying to cut sleep, someone who "oversleeps" should rule out apnea (snoring, obesity, morning headache), depression (low mood, anhedonia), thyroid problems (cold intolerance, weight gain) and anemia (fatigue, pallor, heavy menstruation). Treating the cause often normalizes sleep need. Forcing sleep restriction on an untreated disorder is unsafe.

### Gaps
- ICSD-3 diagnostic criteria and prevalence figures for idiopathic hypersomnia and narcolepsy were not verified from AASM primary documents in this session.

## 8. Ramadan fasting and sleep (BaHammam and colleagues)

### Takeaway
During Ramadan, bedtimes and wake times shift later, and free-living studies often find about 1 h less sleep. When studies control for lifestyle (meal timing, naps, light, work hours), sleep architecture is largely unchanged apart from a consistent **reduction in REM sleep**, and objective daytime sleepiness (MSLT) does not increase. Earlier subjective reports of sleepiness were not confirmed by controlled studies. Most studies are small (often n ≈ 8–16).

### Cited Findings
- **Almeneessier & BaHammam (2018), "How does diurnal intermittent fasting impact sleep, daytime sleepiness, and markers of the biological clock?", *Nature and Science of Sleep* 10:439–452** (narrative review): — [PMC6292409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6292409); [Dove Press](https://www.dovepress.com/how-does-diurnal-intermittent-fasting-impact-sleep-daytime-sleepiness--peer-reviewed-article-NSS)
  - Total sleep time: free-living studies (Roky 2000, 2001; Bogdan 2001) found about 1 h less sleep. Controlled studies (BaHammam 2014; Chamari 2016) found no change. An actigraphy study (BaHammam 2013) found reduced sleep only in fasters.
  - Timing: bedtime and wake time shift later in several Muslim countries, even among non-fasters (BaHammam 2005).
  - Architecture: reduced REM is the only consistent change (Roky 2001; BaHammam 2004; Chamari 2016). In BaHammam 2014, REM fell during fasting both in and outside Ramadan and normalized afterwards. NREM was unchanged.
  - Sleepiness: subjective ESS rose in some studies (BaHammam 2003, n = 56; Benchekroun 1999, n = 264) but not others (BaHammam 2005, n = 101; Margolis & Reed 2004, n = 109; Nugraha 2017, n = 50). Objective MSLT showed no change in controlled studies (BaHammam 2004; BaHammam 2014, n = 8). Reaction-time alertness also showed no decline (BaHammam 2013, n = 16 and n = 8).
  - Uncontrolled daytime naps and late meals confound many studies. Changes seem to come mostly from lifestyle shifts (later work hours, late meals, light at night) rather than from fasting itself.
- A related review: Qasrawi, Pandi-Perumal & BaHammam (2017), "The effect of intermittent fasting during Ramadan on sleep, sleepiness, cognitive function, and circadian rhythm," *Sleep and Breathing*. — [Springer PDF link](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11325-017-1473-x.pdf) [listed; full text not reviewed]

### Inferences
- Ramadan shows that a schedule built on a later night with a pre-dawn meal (suhoor), plus a midday nap, is compatible with normal objective alertness when total sleep is protected through split sleep. This is consistent with the biphasic and split-sleep findings in Section 3, and it does not show that total sleep need falls.
- Practical evidence-based points include eating the evening meal at least 3 h before bed, limiting light and screens late at night, and protecting total 24-h sleep (night plus nap).

### Gaps
- Large, well-controlled Ramadan studies are lacking. Most PSG studies have n ≈ 8.
- Studies specifically on regular voluntary fasting (e.g., Monday/Thursday fasting) and sleep were not found in this session.

## 9. Scientific and academic books on sleep (and on sleeping less)

### Takeaway
The most authoritative academic reference is Kryger's *Principles and Practice of Sleep Medicine*. For the "can I sleep less?" question, the most directly relevant scientist-authored books are Stampi's *Why We Nap* (polyphasic and ultrashort sleep, written for emergencies) and Jim Horne's books (*Why We Sleep*, 1988; *Sleepfaring*, 2006), which argue for a "core" versus "optional" sleep distinction and question the size of the "sleep debt epidemic." Walker's *Why We Sleep* (2017) is popular but has documented factual errors.

### Cited Findings (book list with reliability)
1. **Kryger, Roth, Goldstein (eds.; Dement listed), *Principles and Practice of Sleep Medicine*, 7th ed., Elsevier, Philadelphia, 2022** (released Dec 2021; 2 volumes, about 2,100–2,200 pp, ISBN 9780323661898). The standard clinical and scientific textbook covering sleep need, deprivation, disorders and hypersomnia. Reliability: **very high** (multi-author, peer-level reference). — [Elsevier shop](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/principles-and-practice-of-sleep-medicine-2-volume-set/kryger/978-0-323-66189-8); [British Library catalog](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/021024351)
2. **Claudio Stampi (ed.), *Why We Nap: Evolution, Chronobiology, and Functions of Polyphasic and Ultrashort Sleep*, Birkhäuser, Boston, 1992.** An edited scientific volume on polyphasic and ultrashort sleep: solo sailors, sustained military operations, and nap strategies to limit losses when long sleep is impossible. Ultrashort sleep is presented as an emergency strategy, not a lifestyle replacement. Reliability: **high (academic), but dated**, and it is often misquoted by polyphasic-sleep advocates. — [Wikipedia: Why We Nap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Why_We_Nap); [PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/zeo/1992-stampi-whywenap.pdf)
3. **Jim Horne, *Why We Sleep: The Functions of Sleep in Humans and Other Mammals*, Oxford University Press, 1988.** An academic monograph proposing that the first few hours of sleep ("core sleep," mostly deep NREM) serve essential brain restoration while the remainder is more "optional." Ian Oswald's *Nature* review (1988) noted that this rests heavily on studies of healthy young volunteers. Reliability: **academic but contested and dated**. It is the main scientific source for the argument that some people sleep more than they strictly need. — [Nature review by Oswald (1988)](https://nature.com/articles/333408a0.pdf); [Raconteur interview with Horne](https://www.raconteur.net/understanding-sleep/lullaby-for-a-lifetimes-deep-sleep)
4. **Jim Horne, *Sleepfaring: A Journey Through the Science of Sleep*, Oxford University Press, 2006** (paperback 2007). Horne was Professor of Psychophysiology and Director of the Loughborough Sleep Research Centre. A popular-science book by a leading researcher that challenges "conventional wisdom on the amount of sleep we actually need." Reliability: **good (expert author)**, but it represents a skeptical minority view on sleep-debt alarmism. — [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL7998608W); [PRI story "Why we can relax about sleep"](https://www.pri.org/stories/2008-10-17/why-we-can-relax-about-sleep)
5. **A. Roger Ekirch, *At Day's Close: Night in Times Past*, W.W. Norton, 2005.** A historical study documenting "first" and "second" sleep in pre-industrial Europe. Relevance: segmented sleep, not reduced sleep. Reliability: **high as history**, but its physiological interpretation is debated (Boyce 2023; Yetish 2015). — [Yetish 2015 reference list](https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/yetishetal2015.pdf); [Ekirch research page](https://sites.google.com/vt.edu/roger-ekirch/sleep-research)
6. **Till Roenneberg, *Internal Time: Chronotypes, Social Jet Lag, and Why You're So Tired*, Harvard University Press, 2012.** A chronobiologist (LMU Munich) argues that much "tiredness" is circadian misalignment ("social jet lag"), and that more daylight and timing aligned to chronotype help. Reliability: **good (researcher-author, popular format)**. — [De Gruyter/Harvard UP](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.4159/harvard.9780674065482/html); [The Marginalian review](https://www.themarginalian.org/2012/05/11/internal-time-till-roenneber/)
7. **Russell Foster, *Life Time: The New Science of the Body Clock, and How It Can Revolutionize Your Sleep and Health*, Penguin, 19 May 2022.** Foster is an Oxford Professor of Circadian Neuroscience. The book covers chronotype, light and sleep timing, and notes that sleep need varies with genetics, environment and age. Reliability: **good**. — [Penguin UK](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/321815/life-time-by-foster-russell/9780241993392); [Oxford Neuroscience news](https://www.neuroscience.ox.ac.uk/news/how-to-use-the-science-of-the-body-clock-to-improve-our-sleep-and-health)
8. **Penelope A. Lewis, *The Secret World of Sleep: The Surprising Science of the Mind at Rest*, Palgrave Macmillan, 2013** (paperback 2014). Lewis ran the Sleep and Memory Lab at the University of Manchester. The book covers sleep and memory, dreams, and a chapter on improving sleep. Reliability: **good (researcher-author)**. — [Macmillan](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781137386977); [Publishers Weekly](https://www2.publishersweekly.com/978-0-230-10759-5)
9. **Matthew Walker, *Why We Sleep: Unlocking the Power of Sleep and Dreams*, Scribner/Penguin, 2017.** A best-seller arguing strongly that people need 7–9 h and that short sleep is very harmful. Alexey Guzey's 2019 critique documented numerous factual errors and exaggerations (130 h spent fact-checking one chapter), and statistician Andrew Gelman criticized a graph from which a contradicting bar had been removed. Reliability: **mixed**. Its core message (adults need about 7–9 h) matches the consensus, but specific claims should be checked. — [Guzey critique](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/); [Gelman, Statistical Modeling blog](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2019/11/18/is-matthew-walkers-why-we-sleep-riddled-with-scientific-and-factual-errors/); [Wikipedia: Matthew Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Walker_(scientist))
10. [UNVERIFIED bibliographic details, from prior knowledge] **William C. Dement & Christopher Vaughan, *The Promise of Sleep*, Delacorte Press, 1999.** By the founder of sleep medicine at Stanford, it popularized the concept of "sleep debt." Reliability: **high (pioneer author), dated**.
11. [UNVERIFIED] **Steven W. Lockley & Russell G. Foster, *Sleep: A Very Short Introduction*, Oxford University Press, 2012.** A concise academic overview. Reliability: **high**.
12. [UNVERIFIED] **Sara C. Mednick, *Take a Nap! Change Your Life*, Workman, 2006; *The Power of the Downstate*, Hachette Go, 2022.** A nap researcher's evidence-based popular books on napping and recovery. Reliability: **good (researcher-author), popular format**.
13. [UNVERIFIED] **Stanley Coren, *Sleep Thieves*, Free Press, 1996.** A psychologist argues that society is chronically sleep-deprived. Reliability: **moderate, dated**.
14. Not a book, but a key critique document: **Piotr Woźniak, "Polyphasic Sleep: Facts and Myths" (2005, updated)**, an online essay by the creator of SuperMemo. Reliability: **informed opinion, not peer-reviewed**. — [SuperMemo](https://www.supermemo.com/en/blog/polyphasic)

### Inferences
- The only peer-level academic books that seriously entertain "people can sleep less than they think" are Horne (1988, 2006) and, in a narrow emergency sense, Stampi (1992). Even Horne's argument is about not exceeding need ("optional sleep" beyond about 6–7 h for some people), not about polyphasic 2–4 h schedules.

## Cross-cutting summary for the report writer (evidence-graded)
- [STRONG] Adults need about 7–9 h. Regular ≤6 h causes cumulative cognitive deficits that people do not perceive (Van Dongen 2003; Belenky 2003; AASM/SRS 2015; NSF 2015).
- [STRONG association, uncertain cause] Both <6–7 h and >9 h are associated with higher mortality (Cappuccio 2010: 1.12 / 1.30; Jike 2018: long 1.39). Long sleep is likely a marker of depression, apnea or illness (Grandner & Drummond 2007).
- [MODERATE] Genetic natural short sleepers exist but are extremely rare (e.g., NPSR1 about 1 in 4 million). Most self-described short sleepers carry sleep debt (Klerman & Dijk 2005). There is no evidence that sleep need can be trained down.
- [STRONG, negative] Polyphasic schedules that cut total sleep have no evidence of benefit and are associated with harm (NSF panel, Weaver 2021).
- [MODERATE] Biphasic sleep with the same total (night block plus a 1–1.5 h nap) performs similarly to, or slightly better than, consolidated sleep for some cognitive measures in teens (Cousins 2019; Lo 2019), with a possible glucose cost.
- [MODERATE] Short naps (10–20 min) are the most efficient. A caffeine-plus-short-nap combination works for acute sleepiness. Naps of 60–90 min aid learning but cause inertia, and habitual naps >60 min are associated with worse health.
- [MODERATE] The safe way to reduce excess time in bed is to improve sleep efficiency (SRT-style time-in-bed compression to about 85–90% efficiency, a fixed wake time, light, exercise, no caffeine after mid-afternoon, no alcohol), aiming for 7–8 h of actual sleep rather than below 7 h.
- [MODERATE] In Ramadan, controlled studies show sleep timing shifts and REM falls, but objective sleepiness does not increase when total sleep is protected (BaHammam studies).
- Safety: anyone who sleeps >9–10 h and is still tired, snores, or is sleepy while driving should get a medical evaluation before trying to reduce sleep.
