## 1. HAV: transmission, incubation, infectious period, stool shedding, household secondary attack rate

### Takeaway
HAV spreads faecal-orally; the patient is most infectious in the ~2 weeks BEFORE jaundice appears and for about 1 week after, so by the time the husband is visibly jaundiced most of the household exposure has already happened. Without prophylaxis, reported household secondary attack rates range from ~6% (one RCT) to 15–30% (CDC-cited range).

### Cited Findings
- Incubation: "The mean incubation period of hepatitis A is approximately 28 days (range 15–50 days)" **[quoted]** — [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis A](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)
- Peak infectiousness: people are "most infectious 1–2 weeks before onset of illness" **[quoted]** — [CDC Pink Book, Ch. 9 Hepatitis A (2021 edition)](https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-9-hepatitis-a.html)
- High concentrations of virus appear in stool "2–3 weeks before and 1 week after onset of illness" **[quoted]** — [CDC Manual for the Surveillance of Vaccine-Preventable Diseases, Ch. 3 Hepatitis A](https://www.cdc.gov/surv-manual/php/table-of-contents/chapter-3-hepatitis-a.html)
- Operational infectious period used by a US state health department: two weeks before to one week after onset of jaundice (or of elevated liver enzymes) **[summary]** — [New Jersey DOH Hepatitis A chapter](https://nj.gov/health/cd/documents/chapters/hepa_ch.pdf)
- Pink Book: virus excretion "may continue for up to 3 weeks after onset of symptoms" **[summary]** — [CDC Pink Book Ch. 9](https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-9-hepatitis-a.html)
- Household secondary attack rate without PEP: "secondary attack rates of 15%–30% have been reported in households, with higher rates of transmission occurring from infected young children than from infected adolescents and adults" **[quoted by search as CDC/ACIP text; exact CDC page not opened — most likely the ACIP 2020 recommendations]** — [ACIP 2020, MMWR Recomm Rep 69(RR-5)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/rr/rr6905a1.htm)
- Lower figure from a randomized trial (Italy): in unvaccinated households, secondary infections occurred in 12 of 207 (5.8%) household contacts **[summary]** — [Sagliocca et al., Lancet 1999, "Efficacy of hepatitis A vaccine in prevention of secondary hepatitis A infection"](https://www.ovid.com/journals/lanc/fulltext/00005531-199904030-00011~efficacy-of-hepatitis-a-vaccine-in-prevention-of-secondary)
- An Amsterdam cohort paper states that secondary attack rate among contacts is "up to 50%" (upper bound; not limited to households without PEP) **[summary]** — [PMC2870411](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2870411)
- Texas guidance: the patient "should not handle food for other people for 1 week after onset of jaundice" **[summary]** — [Texas DSHS Hepatitis A investigation guideline 2023](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/sites/default/files/IDCU/investigation/electronic/EAIDG/2023/Hepatitis-A.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because peak shedding precedes jaundice, hygiene measures started at jaundice onset reduce but cannot eliminate risk; the PEP clock (14 days from last exposure during the infectious period) is the time-critical item.
- The practical "infectious until" date to use for the husband is ~7 days after the day jaundice began (if the cause is HAV); sources vary between 1 and 3 weeks, so a cautious household could extend strict precautions to 2–3 weeks.
- Adults transmit less efficiently than young children (CDC text above), so the husband-to-household risk is at the lower end of reported ranges — but an infant who becomes infected is then a more efficient and longer-lasting source for the mother (see section 3).

### Gaps
- I could not open the CDC/WHO pages to confirm verbatim wording; the 15–30% figure should be checked against the ACIP 2020 text.
- WHO hepatitis A fact sheet figures (incubation "14–28 days", etc.) were not retrieved in this session.

## 2. HAV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) — infant <12 months, adults, pregnant women; availability in Bangladesh

### Takeaway
Established US guidance (ACIP 2018 update, carried into ACIP 2020): PEP within 2 weeks of exposure; infants <12 months get immune globulin (IG) 0.1 mL/kg and NOT vaccine; everyone ≥12 months gets a single dose of hepatitis A vaccine; adults >40 may additionally get IG at the provider's discretion; pregnant women are treated like non-pregnant women. In Bangladesh, hepatitis A vaccine is sold on the private market (several brands) but is not in the public EPI schedule, and I found no evidence either way on availability of intramuscular IG — which is the only ACIP-recommended PEP product for the infant.

### Cited Findings
**ACIP / CDC (USA)**
- Timing: unvaccinated recently exposed persons should receive PEP "as soon as possible, within 2 weeks of exposure" **[summary]** — [ACIP update, MMWR 2018;67(43):1216–1220](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/wr/mm6743a5.htm)
- Infants <12 months: the ACIP 2020 PEP table lists healthy infants aged 0–11 months as receiving IG 0.1 mL/kg body weight and no hepatitis A vaccine for PEP **[summary]** — [reproduction of ACIP 2020 table, PMC8344968 Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8344968/table/T4); original: [MMWR Recomm Rep 2020;69(RR-5)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/rr/rr6905a1.htm)
- IG dose was raised (2017) to 0.1 mL/kg for PEP because of lower anti-HAV potency of current IG lots **[summary]** — [Updated Dosing Instructions for Immune Globulin (Human) GamaSTAN S/D for HAV Prophylaxis, MMWR 2017 (PMC5657912)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5657912/)
- Persons ≥12 months: ACIP (Feb 2018) recommends hepatitis A vaccine for PEP for all persons aged ≥12 months; for persons >40 years IG may be administered in addition, depending on the provider's risk assessment. Evidence for vaccine PEP in >40s was graded low (GRADE evidence type 4) **[summary]** — [MMWR 2018;67(43)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/wr/mm6743a5.htm); same content at [PMC6319798](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6319798)
- IG and live vaccines: MMR should not be administered for ≥2 weeks before and 6 months after IG (ACIP 2020 table footnote) **[quoted]** — [PMC8344968 Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8344968/table/T4)
- Pregnancy: "pregnant women should receive PEP for the same indications as nonpregnant women"; it "may be reasonable to offer IG in addition to vaccine, particularly if the woman is a household or sexual contact of a case" **[summary; state document adapted from California DPH]** — [Maine CDC Hep A PEP guidance](https://www.maine.gov/dhhs/mecdc/infectious-disease/epi/hepatitis/documents/Hep%20A%20PEP_new.pdf)
- Pregnancy safety: no increase in maternal or infant adverse events has been observed after hepatitis A vaccination or IG administration in pregnancy **[summary]** — [CDC Hepatitis A clinical care page](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-care/index.html)
- Efficacy context: in the Lancet 1999 RCT, vaccinating household contacts reduced secondary infection versus 5.8% (12/207) in unvaccinated households **[summary; vaccinated-arm count not retrieved]** — [Sagliocca et al. 1999](https://www.ovid.com/journals/lanc/fulltext/00005531-199904030-00011~efficacy-of-hepatitis-a-vaccine-in-prevention-of-secondary)

**Other national guidance (for comparison)**
- UK: "the mainstay of hepatitis A post-exposure prophylaxis in non-immune close contacts is hepatitis A vaccine"; human normal immunoglobulin (HNIG) "may provide short term immunity in the first 7 days" and is most effective soon after exposure **[summary]** — [UKHSA, Hepatitis A immunoglobulin (issued 2024)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/immunoglobulin-when-to-use/hepatitis-a-immunoglobulin-issued-2024)
- Australia: PEP should be arranged as soon as possible for susceptible contacts if within two weeks of contact **[summary]** — [CDNA national guidelines for public health units: Hepatitis A (2025)](https://www.health.gov.au/sites/default/files/2025-04/hepatitis-a-cdna-national-guidelines-for-public-health-units.pdf)

**WHO**
- WHO position paper on hepatitis A vaccines published in the Weekly Epidemiological Record 7 Oct 2022 (replaces 2012 paper); inactivated vaccines can be given as single- or two-dose schedules; live attenuated vaccines should not be used in pregnancy (theoretical risk); annexes include GRADE tables on vaccine for post-exposure prophylaxis **[summary — the PEP wording itself was NOT retrieved]** — [WHO WER 2022;97(40):493–512](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/who-wer9740-493-512); [SAGE summary PDF](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/immunization/position_paper_documents/hepatitis/sage_hepatitisa_positionpaper_summary_2022.pdf)

**Bangladesh availability**
- Hepatitis A vaccine brands listed for sale by Bangladeshi online pharmacies / drug indexes (prices as listed at time of search, Nov 2026; they change): PrevaHAV (Incepta) adult 1 mL ≈ ৳1,000 (Arogga ৳959.50), PrevaHAV paediatric 0.5 mL ≈ ৳600 (Arogga ৳540); Avaxim 80 U paediatric (Synovia Pharma) ≈ ৳1,520 (other sites ৳1,444–1,568); Havrix 720 Junior ≈ ৳686.89 **[summary]** — [Medex vaccine listing](https://medex.com.bd/index.php/special-segments/vaccine); [Arogga PrevaHAV adult](https://www.arogga.com/product/23998/prevahav-for-adult-injection-1ml); [Arogga PrevaHAV paediatric](https://www.arogga.com/product/15365/prevahav-for-pediatric-injection-0-5ml); [Arogga Havrix 720 Junior](https://www.arogga.com/product/8989/havrix-720-junior-injection-720eu-0-5ml); [Arogga Havrix 1440 Adult](https://www.arogga.com/product/8988/havrix-1440-adult0-injection-1440eu-ml); [ePharma Avaxim paediatric](https://epharma.com.bd/en/medicines/vaccines/avaxim-pediatric-injection-80-unit05ml-3746)
- Vaccination offered at private diagnostic chains, e.g. "Havrix hepatitis A single" listed at Popular Diagnostic Centre, Dhanmondi **[search hit title only]** — [sasthyaseba listing](https://sasthyaseba.com/hospitals/popular-diagnostic-centre-ltd-dhanmondi/diagnostics/havrix-hepatitis-a-single)
- EPI: Bangladesh's routine programme gives vaccines against 10 diseases from birth to 15 months **[summary]** — [Jagonews24, "Child immunisation: When and which to give"](https://www.jagonews24.com/en/national/news/91363); typhoid conjugate vaccine being integrated into routine immunization from 2026 — [UNICEF Bangladesh press release](https://www.unicef.org/bangladesh/en/press-releases/bangladesh-launches-nationwide-typhoid-conjugate-vaccine-campaign-protect-50-million); programme reference — [WHO Bangladesh EPI factsheet 2024](https://www.who.int/bangladesh/about-us/publications/i/item/bangladesh-epi-factsheet-2024)
- A 2026 commentary in the BSMMU Journal, "Time to reconsider hepatitis A vaccination policy in Bangladesh", argues that the epidemiological shift "necessitates a reassessment of hepatitis A vaccination policy" and that EPI "provides a robust delivery platform" — i.e. hepatitis A vaccine is being proposed for, not already in, public policy **[summary]** — [BSMMUJ commentary](https://www.banglajol.info/index.php/BSMMUJ/article/download/87270/57581/245512?inline=1)

### Inferences
- Hepatitis A vaccine is not part of Bangladesh EPI (inferred from the 10-disease EPI description plus the 2026 commentary calling for policy reconsideration; no source found that lists it in EPI). It is obtainable privately in cities.
- For the wife (adult, likely <40): ACIP-style PEP = one dose of inactivated hepatitis A vaccine within 14 days of last exposure; this is realistic in urban Bangladesh (private purchase ~৳1,000). Pregnancy or breastfeeding is not a contraindication to inactivated vaccine per CDC/state guidance above. However, most Bangladeshi adults are already immune (section 8), so an anti-HAV IgG/total antibody test, if quickly available, could show PEP is unnecessary — but testing should not delay PEP beyond the 14-day window (inference).
- For the infant <12 months: ACIP's only recommended product is IG 0.1 mL/kg IM. ACIP's vaccine recommendation for infants 6–11 months applies to pre-travel protection, not PEP (this specific point is from my prior knowledge of the 2018 MMWR whose title covers "Preexposure Prophylaxis for International Travel"; the search extract did not quote it — verify). If IG cannot be sourced in Bangladesh, the fallback is strict hygiene plus protecting the caregivers (vaccinating the mother so she does not become a second source), and paediatric consultation.
- All paediatric hepatitis A vaccine products listed in Bangladesh are licensed from 12 months of age (standard labelling; not verified per-product here).

### Gaps
- **IG availability in Bangladesh: no source found.** I found no Bangladeshi source showing that intramuscular human normal immunoglobulin (GamaSTAN equivalent) is marketed or stocked; searches returned only UK/US/Australian documents. Must be checked with a paediatrician / DGDA / large hospital pharmacies. (IVIG is a different product and not an ACIP-recommended substitute — from memory, unverified.)
- WHO 2022 position paper's exact PEP statement (vaccine vs IG, time window, infants) not retrieved.
- From memory, unverified this session: UK (UKHSA 2017) hepatitis A guidance handles infants by vaccinating all carers/nappy-changers (infants <2 months) or offering off-label vaccine / vaccinating carers (2–11 months), specifically because IG is scarce. If true this is the most relevant model for a setting without IG — worth verifying at [gov.uk hepatitis A guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/hepatitis-a/hepatitis-a).
- Whether the infant is protected by maternal antibody (likely if the mother is immune from childhood infection) — not sourced this session.
- No Bangladesh national (DGHS/IEDCR) guideline on hepatitis A contact prophylaxis was found.

## 3. HAV in infants: severity and shedding

### Takeaway
Infant/young-child HAV infection is usually silent or mild (about 70% of infections under age 6 are asymptomatic and jaundice is uncommon), but infected infants can shed virus far longer than adults — up to months — making an infected infant a hidden source for the mother and others via diapers.

### Cited Findings
- "In children younger than 6, 70% of infections are asymptomatic. When symptoms do present, young children typically do not have jaundice, whereas most older children and adults with HAV infection have jaundice." **[quoted]** — [CDC Surveillance Manual Ch. 3](https://www.cdc.gov/surv-manual/php/table-of-contents/chapter-3-hepatitis-a.html)
- "In older children and adults, infection is usually symptomatic, with jaundice occurring in more than 70% of patients." **[quoted]** — [CDC Pink Book Ch. 9](https://www.cdc.gov/pinkbook/hcp/table-of-contents/chapter-9-hepatitis-a.html)
- Conflicting (higher) figure: 90% of children <5 years with HAV infection are asymptomatic, whereas 90% of adults have symptoms **[summary]** — [Healio Vaccine Handbook, hepatitis A clinical features](https://www.healio.com/clinical-guidance/vaccine-handbook/hepatitis-a/clinical-features-overview)
- Shedding in children: "infants and children can shed virus up to 6 months after infection" **[summary]** — [CDC Yellow Book (2026 ed.), Hepatitis A](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-associated-infections-diseases/hepatitis-a.html)
- Children can shed HAV longer than adults, up to ten weeks after onset of clinical illness; prolonged excretion (up to six months) occasionally documented in premature infants and the severely immunosuppressed **[summary]** — [New Jersey DOH Hepatitis A chapter](https://nj.gov/health/cd/documents/chapters/hepa_ch.pdf)
- Transmission is more efficient from infected young children than from adults (see 15–30% household attack-rate quote) — [ACIP 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/rr/rr6905a1.htm)

### Inferences
- The main reason to protect the infant from HAV is not usually the infant's own risk of severe disease (low) but (a) rare severe cases and (b) the infant becoming a prolonged, asymptomatic source for a non-immune mother/other children.
- Diaper hygiene should continue for weeks to months if the infant may have been infected, even if the infant looks well.

### Gaps
- No infant-specific (<12 months) case-fatality or hospitalization figure was retrieved.
- Sources disagree on duration of shedding in children (3 weeks / 10 weeks / up to 6 months).
- No data found on HAV transmission via breast milk; breastfeeding guidance for a mother exposed to/infected with HAV not retrieved (commonly stated as "continue breastfeeding with hand hygiene" — unverified this session).

## 4. HEV: transmission, person-to-person spread, incubation, shedding, vaccine, PEP

### Takeaway
HEV in South Asia is overwhelmingly waterborne (faecally contaminated drinking water); household person-to-person spread is consistently described as uncommon/minor in Indian studies, though not impossible. A vaccine (Hecolin/HEV 239) exists but is licensed only in China (2011) and Pakistan (2020), is not WHO-prequalified, and was only used in Bangladesh inside a trial; no post-exposure prophylaxis exists in practice.

### Cited Findings
**Incubation and shedding**
- WHO (fact sheet, updated April 2025 per search): "The incubation period following exposure to HEV ranges from 2 to 10 weeks, with an average of 5 to 6 weeks"; infected persons excrete "the virus beginning from a few days before to 3–4 weeks after onset of the disease" **[quoted]** — [WHO Hepatitis E fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-e)
- CDC Yellow Book: incubation 2–9 weeks (mean 6 weeks) **[summary]** — [CDC Yellow Book, Hepatitis E](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/yellowbook/2024/infections-diseases/hepatitis-e); CDC basics page: symptoms usually appear 2–6 weeks after exposure — [CDC Hepatitis E Basics](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-e/about/)
- Other figures: 15–60 days, mean 40 days; infectious from ~1 week before onset; prolonged shedding reported in convalescence/chronic infection **[summary]** — [California DPH HEV guidance](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CID/DCDC/CDPH%20Document%20Library/IDBGuidanceforCALHJs-HEVInfection.pdf); HEV detected in stool 14 days after jaundice onset — [Iowa HHS epi manual: Hepatitis E](https://hhs.iowa.gov/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis-e)
- NSW Health: people are normally infectious for around 2 weeks after symptoms start, but virus has been detected up to 6 weeks **[summary]** — [NSW Health Hepatitis E fact sheet](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/Infectious/factsheets/Pages/Hepatitis_E.aspx)
- WHO: cases are not clinically distinguishable from other acute viral hepatitis; no specific antiviral treatment changes the course of acute hepatitis E **[summary]** — [WHO Hepatitis E fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-e)

**Person-to-person / household transmission**
- Kanpur, India epidemic: among 343 families, 111 hepatitis cases; 22 "later" cases occurred within 2 weeks of the index case (too soon for intrafamilial transmission); 103 of 111 (92.8%) were attributed to primary waterborne infection; conclusion: intrafamilial transmission plays a minor role compared with continued water contamination **[summary of abstract]** — [Aggarwal & Naik, J Hepatol 1994;21:718–723](https://repository.ias.ac.in/94808)
- Sporadic hepatitis E (India): serological study of household contacts concluded person-to-person transmission is uncommon in sporadic hepatitis E **[summary]** — [Somani et al., J Viral Hepat 2003](https://repository.ias.ac.in/94612)
- UK guidance: "coincidental infections within households do occur, however person-to-person transmissions are rare" **[quoted]** — [Public Health Operational Guidelines for Hepatitis E (PHE)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/845090/Public_Health_Operational_Guidelines_for_Hepatitis_E-protection_response.pdf)
- Counter-evidence (Africa): large outbreak in northern Uganda — "high attack rate in households, lack of a common source of infection, and poor hygienic practices" pointed to person-to-person spread **[summary]** — [Teshale et al., Clin Infect Dis 2010 (PubMed 20178415)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20178415/)
- Rural Bangladesh: household surveillance in a community of ~23,500 people over 22 months "raised the novel possibility of parenteral and person-to-person transmission of HEV in non-epidemic, sporadic disease settings" (a hypothesis, not a measured attack rate) **[summary; URL attribution probable, not confirmed]** — [Labrique et al., PLoS One 2013 (PMC3652836)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3652836)
- Bangladesh outbreaks reviewed were all attributed to faecally contaminated water supply in the rainy season or hot summer months **[summary]** — [Viruses 2023;15(1):63](https://mdpi.com/1999-4915/15/1/63/html)

**Vaccine**
- Hecolin (HEV 239, Xiamen Innovax) licensed by China's regulator in December 2011; available in China since October 2012 **[summary]** — [PMC4324226](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4324226); [Gavi vaccine profile: hepatitis E](https://gavi.org/vaccineswork/vaccine-profiles-hepatitis-e)
- "Beyond China, the vaccine has been licensed in Pakistan in 2020, and registration processes are ongoing in other countries (India, Nepal and Indonesia)"; Hecolin "has not yet been submitted for WHO Prequalification"; manufacturer intends to submit a new formulation with a 2026–2027 timeline **[quoted/summary; status as of March 2024]** — [WHO SAGE hepatitis E background paper, March 2024](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/immunization/sage/2024/march/hepatitis_e_background_paper_sage_mar24.pdf)
- WHO position (2015 position paper): not recommended for routine national programmes at that time, but should be considered in outbreak response; SAGE March 2024 recommended that a two-dose schedule could be used in fragile, conflict-affected and vulnerable settings **[summary]** — [WHO hepatitis E position paper page](https://www.who.int/teams/immunization-vaccines-and-biologicals/policies/position-papers/hepatitis-e); [SAGE background paper 2024](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/immunization/sage/2024/march/hepatitis_e_background_paper_sage_mar24.pdf)
- First reactive mass campaign: Bentiu, South Sudan, from March 2022 (MSF + MoH); ~25,000 people including pregnant women received vaccine in the first two rounds **[summary]** — [MSF press release](https://www.doctorswithoutborders.org/latest/south-sudan-msf-carries-out-worlds-first-reactive-vaccination-campaign-against-hepatitis-e)
- **Matlab, Bangladesh trial (icddr,b / Norwegian Institute of Public Health; NCT02759991)**: phase 4, double-blind, cluster-randomised; 67 villages; 19,460 women enrolled Oct 2017–Feb 2019; HEV239 n=9,478 (33 clusters) vs hepatitis B vaccine control n=9,982 (34 clusters). No pregnant participant had confirmed hepatitis E in either arm; in non-pregnant women, 6 control (HBV-vaccine) recipients had hepatitis E. Effectiveness in pregnancy "remains uncertain". Miscarriage was higher in the HEV239 arm: 136/2,407 (5.7%) vs 102/2,604 (3.9%), adjusted OR 1.54 **[summary of Lancet Global Health 2024 abstract]** — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02759991](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02759991); [Oslo University Hospital research news on the Lancet GH publication](https://www.ous-research.no/no/ous/news/25461); companion fetal-loss analysis: Lancet Glob Health 2024;12(8):e1300–e1311, doi 10.1016/S2214-109X(24)00193-1
- WHO GACVS: the Bangladesh trial "indicated a higher rate of spontaneous abortion among recipients around pregnancy"; the signal could not be ruled out and further analysis was requested **[summary]** — [WHO GACVS: Safety of hepatitis E vaccines](https://www.who.int/groups/global-advisory-committee-on-vaccine-safety/topics/hepatitis-e-vaccines)
- 2026 follow-up of 1,480 women from the Matlab trial: at 2 years seropositivity 83.7% in HEV-vaccinated vs 51.6% in controls **[summary]** — [PubMed 41763639](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41763639/)

### Inferences
- If the husband's jaundice is HEV, the family's main shared risk is the common water/food source that infected him, not contact with him; the wife and infant have probably been drinking the same water. The highest-yield action is making all drinking water safe immediately, with standard stool/hand hygiene as a secondary layer.
- A reasonable cautious shedding window for hygiene precautions with HEV is until ~3–4 weeks after jaundice onset (WHO), acknowledging shorter (2 weeks) and longer (6 weeks) figures.
- No HEV vaccine is licensed or commercially available in Bangladesh as of the latest information found (March 2024 SAGE paper lists only China and Pakistan); no source describes any effective HEV post-exposure prophylaxis. So for HEV there is no PEP for the wife or infant — prevention is water, sanitation and hygiene only.
- Even if Hecolin were available, the unresolved miscarriage signal makes it unsuitable to recommend to a possibly-pregnant wife; and it is licensed only for ages ≥16 (from memory, unverified), so not for an infant.

### Gaps
- No measured household secondary attack rate for HEV from Bangladesh was found. The often-quoted "0.7–2.2% in household contacts of HEV vs 50–75% for HAV" figure could not be confirmed in the retrieved abstracts.
- Whether immune globulin is ineffective for HEV (commonly stated by CDC) was not retrieved in this session — treat "no PEP exists" as an inference from absence of any recommendation.
- HEV genotype in Bangladesh (genotype 1 predominant) was not explicitly confirmed by a retrieved quote; CDC Yellow Book refers to "areas endemic for HEV-1".
- Current (late 2026) regulatory status of Hecolin in Bangladesh/DGDA and WHO prequalification not found; latest data point is March 2024.
- HEV239-arm case count among non-pregnant women in the Matlab trial not retrieved (only the 6 control cases).

## 5. HEV in pregnancy: mortality, fetal outcomes, Bangladesh evidence; what a possibly-pregnant wife should do

### Takeaway
Pregnancy is the single greatest risk factor for fatal hepatitis E: WHO states up to 20–25% of women infected in the third trimester can die, and Bangladeshi data attribute a substantial share of maternal deaths to jaundice/hepatitis-like illness. A possibly-pregnant wife sharing the husband's water source is the highest-priority person to protect and should be tested for pregnancy, drink only boiled water, and seek care immediately if any symptoms appear.

### Cited Findings
- WHO: "Pregnant women with hepatitis E, particularly those in the second or third trimester, are at increased risk of acute liver failure, fetal loss and mortality. Up to 20–25% of pregnant women can die if they get hepatitis E in third trimester." **[quoted]** — [WHO Hepatitis E fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-e)
- Published pregnancy case-fatality range 7%–40% (from earlier sporadic cases and outbreaks) **[summary]** — [Labrique et al., Emerg Infect Dis 2012;18(9)](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/18/9/12-0241)
- Other stated ranges: 10–30%, particularly third trimester — [Iowa HHS](https://hhs.iowa.gov/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis-e); "30% or higher" in a review — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-981-13-0050-9_14) **[summaries]**
- CDC: in areas endemic for HEV-1, infection in a pregnant woman can progress to liver failure and death **[summary]** — [CDC Yellow Book, Hepatitis E](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/yellowbook/2024/infections-diseases/hepatitis-e)
- **Bangladesh, hospital surveillance (6 tertiary hospitals, 2014–2017)**: HEV was a major (34%) cause of acute jaundice; overall case fatality of acute HEV in hospital was 5%, but 12% among pregnant women; cases occurred throughout the year **[summary]** — [Paul et al., bioRxiv 2019 / PLoS NTD 2020](https://biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2019/07/02/688721.full-text.pdf)
- **Bangladesh, rural north-west (2001–2007)**: 1,091 deaths among women of reproductive age, 245 pregnancy-related; hepatitis-like illness accounted for 24/245 (9.8%) of pregnancy-related deaths (and 81/846, 9.6%, of non-pregnancy deaths) **[quoted/summary]** — [Labrique et al., EID 2012](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/18/9/12-0241)
- **Bangladesh, national estimate**: 19%–25% of all maternal deaths and 7%–13% of all neonatal deaths were associated with jaundice in pregnant women **[summary]** — [Gurley et al., Am J Public Health 2012 (PubMed 23078501)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23078501/); press summary: [ReliefWeb, "Hepatitis E: A preventable cause of one in five maternal deaths in Bangladesh"](https://reliefweb.int/report/bangladesh/hepatitis-e-preventable-cause-one-five-maternal-deaths-bangladesh)
- National maternal mortality surveys: jaundice-associated maternal deaths 19% (2001) and 23% (2010); verbal autopsy cannot say how many were HEV; literature links ~58% of jaundice deaths in pregnancy to HEV **[summary]** — [Shah, Nahar, Gurley et al. (LSHTM repository)](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/2535854)
- **Dhaka urban outbreak 2008–2009**: increased maternal and neonatal mortality; "miscarriage and perinatal mortality was 2.7 times higher (95% CI 1.2–6.1) in pregnancies complicated by jaundice"; case-patients who took paracetamol during illness were more likely to die **[quoted/summary]** — [Gurley et al., Clin Infect Dis 2014;59(5):658](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/5/658/2895602)
- Vertical transmission: neonates of HEV-infected mothers "are at risk of vertical transmission, which can result in acute liver failure and high neonatal mortality" **[summary]** — [MSF Medical Guidelines (content updated Feb 2026)](https://medicalguidelines.msf.org/en/node/2331)

### Inferences
- "What the wife should do" (synthesised, not a quoted guideline): (1) do a pregnancy test now; (2) from today drink and brush teeth only with boiled (or otherwise reliably treated) water and avoid street food/raw salads/uncooked foods washed in tap water for at least the HEV incubation window (up to 10 weeks after the last shared exposure); (3) if pregnant, tell her obstetric provider about the household jaundice case and seek urgent care for fever, vomiting, dark urine, yellow eyes, itching or drowsiness; (4) avoid self-medication, including high-dose paracetamol and herbal/kabiraji remedies (the Dhaka outbreak association with paracetamol is observational, but hepatotoxic drugs are a plausible hazard); (5) no vaccine or PEP is available.
- Determining whether the husband has HAV or HEV (anti-HAV IgM, anti-HEV IgM; both widely done in Bangladeshi diagnostic labs) materially changes the plan: HAV → time-limited PEP opportunity; HEV → water safety and pregnancy vigilance.
- If HAV rather than HEV: hepatitis A in pregnancy is not associated with the HEV-type excess mortality (no source retrieved; see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No trimester-specific case-fatality figure from Bangladesh was found; 20–25% (WHO) and 7–40% (literature) are global/historical.
- The paracetamol finding is from one outbreak investigation; causal status unknown.
- No Bangladesh DGHS/OGSB guidance for pregnant household contacts of hepatitis E cases was found.
- HAV-in-pregnancy outcome data not retrieved.

## 6. HEV in infants and breastfeeding

### Takeaway
Evidence is thin. HEV in young children is generally mild or asymptomatic; the documented danger to babies is vertical (mother-to-fetus/newborn) transmission when the mother is infected in pregnancy, not household contact. HEV RNA has been found in colostrum, but transmission through breastfeeding has not been documented and MSF recommends continuing breastfeeding; one review advises against breastfeeding only while the mother herself has symptomatic acute hepatitis E.

### Cited Findings
- Children in endemic areas are frequently exposed early in life, "typically showing mild symptoms or remaining asymptomatic"; many cases go undiagnosed and the true paediatric burden is "largely unknown" **[summary]** — [Clinical and Experimental Hepatology review, "Hepatitis E in the pediatric population: an overlooked disease but a genuine concern"](https://www.termedia.pl/Hepatitis-E-in-the-pediatric-population-An-overlooked-disease-but-a-genuine-concern-,80,56146,1,1.html)
- Clinical hepatitis E involves adults more often than young children; overall mortality ~1% **[summary]** — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-981-13-0050-9_14)
- Vertical transmission is documented and can cause neonatal hepatitis, acute liver failure and high neonatal mortality (small outbreak/hospital series from India and Sudan) **[summary]** — [MSF Medical Guidelines, updated Feb 2026](https://medicalguidelines.msf.org/en/node/2331); [StatPearls: Hepatitis E (NBK532278)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532278/)
- Breastfeeding: small prospective study found anti-HEV antibody and HEV RNA in colostrum of infected mothers, "but breast-feeding appears to be safe for these infants"; authors said the report must be confirmed by others **[quoted/summary]** — [Chibber et al., Arch Gynecol Obstet 2004](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00404-002-0466-5)
- MSF guideline recommends breastfeeding for infants of HEV-infected mothers **[summary]** — [MSF Medical Guidelines](https://medicalguidelines.msf.org/en/node/2331)
- Transmission through breastfeeding "has not been documented" **[summary; advocacy-oriented lactation database, last update 14 July 2023]** — [e-lactancia: Hepatitis E](https://e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/hepatitis-e/product/)
- A review states breastfeeding is considered safe in asymptomatic HEV-infected women but "not advisable" when the mother has symptomatic infection; data on milk transmission are insufficient **[summary]** — [PMC4501603](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501603)
- European serosurvey: anti-HEV prevalence 0.4% in infants under 2 years, rising to 1.5% at 15–17 years (not South Asian data) **[summary]** — [PMC8687073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8687073)

### Inferences
- In this household the mother is not (yet) infected; breastfeeding should continue. Exclusive breastfeeding in an infant under 6 months is itself protective because it avoids the contaminated-water route (inference; WHO infant-feeding logic, not a quoted HEV source). For an infant on formula or complementary foods, water for feeds and for washing bottles/utensils must be boiled.
- If the mother later develops acute hepatitis E, the breastfeeding decision becomes an individual clinical one (sources conflict: MSF says continue; one review says not advisable while symptomatic).

### Gaps
- No South Asian or Bangladeshi data on severity of HEV specifically in infants <1 year acquired postnatally.
- No WHO or CDC statement on breastfeeding with HEV was retrieved.
- No data on father-to-infant household transmission of HEV.

## 7. Practical household measures (hand hygiene, food, water, disinfection, diapers, laundry, utensils)

### Takeaway
The same faecal-oral precautions cover both viruses: soap handwashing at key times, the patient not preparing food for others, boiled drinking water, bleach disinfection of the toilet and high-touch surfaces, careful diaper handling, and no sharing of towels/utensils. HAV is hardy (needs >85°C for 1 minute or 1:100 bleach); HEV heat data are inconsistent, so a full rolling boil is the practical standard.

### Cited Findings
- Handwashing key times: "thoroughly washing hands after using the bathroom, changing diapers and before preparing or eating food, plays an important role in preventing the spread of hepatitis A" **[quoted]** — [Louisiana Dept of Health Hepatitis A public information](https://ldh.la.gov/assets/oph/Center-PHCH/Center-CH/infectious-epi/PublicInfo/HepatitisAPublicInfo.pdf)
- The patient "should not prepare food for others, and practice good handwashing after toileting"; "persons assisting a patient with toileting should practice good hand washing and wash the patient's hands after toileting" **[quoted/summary]** — [Iowa HHS epi manual: Hepatitis A — controlling](https://hhs.iowa.gov/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis/controlling)
- Duration of food-handling restriction: patient should not handle food for others for 1 week after onset of jaundice **[summary]** — [Texas DSHS guideline 2023](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/sites/default/files/IDCU/investigation/electronic/EAIDG/2023/Hepatitis-A.pdf)
- Sharing utensils, towels or personal items with others is flagged as a risk activity **[summary]** — [NSW Health Hepatitis A control guideline](https://www.health.nsw.gov.au/Infectious/controlguideline/Documents/hepatitisa.pdf)
- HAV inactivation: "Heating foods at temperatures greater than 185°F (>85°C) for one minute or disinfecting surfaces with a 1:100 dilution of sodium hypochlorite (i.e., household bleach) in tap water is necessary to inactivate HAV" **[quoted]** — [New Jersey DOH Hepatitis A chapter](https://nj.gov/health/cd/documents/chapters/hepa_ch.pdf)
- Stronger outbreak-style recipe: 5,000 ppm chlorine ≈ 1⅔ cups household bleach per gallon of water (≈ 1 part bleach : 9 parts water), 1 minute contact time then rinse; use within a short time of mixing (sources differ: within 1 hour vs up to 24 hours; contact 1 vs 1–2 minutes) **[summary]** — [Chicago DPH HAV Disinfection tip sheet](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/cdph/infectious_disease/Immunizations/HAV_Disinfection_TipSheet_12182017.pdf); [Sacramento County guideline](https://emd.saccounty.gov/Documents/HEP%20A%20DISINFECTION%20GUIDELINES%2010%2020%2017.pdf); [Maine CDC "cleaning to kill hep A"](https://www.maine.gov/dhhs/mecdc/infectious-disease/epi/hepatitis/documents/cleaning%20to%20kill%20hep%20a.pdf)
- "Most cleaning products do not kill Hepatitis A virus" (i.e., ordinary detergents/phenyl-type cleaners are not sufficient; use chlorine bleach) **[summary]** — [Maine CDC](https://www.maine.gov/dhhs/mecdc/infectious-disease/epi/hepatitis/documents/cleaning%20to%20kill%20hep%20a.pdf)
- Boiling water — WHO: "Heating water to a rolling boil, as recommended in the WHO Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality, is sufficient to inactivate pathogenic bacteria, viruses and protozoa"; WHO says water may be removed from heat once a rolling boil is reached, whereas other agencies recommend maintaining it for 1 minute **[quoted/summary]** — [WHO technical brief "Boil water" (WHO/FWC/WSH/15.02)](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-FWC-WSH-15.02)
- CDC: bring water to a full rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft); boiling does not remove chemicals **[summary]** — [CDC household water treatment](https://www.cdc.gov/global-water-sanitation-hygiene/about/about-household-water-treatment.html)
- HEV heat resistance: heating food to an internal 71°C for 20 minutes was needed to fully inactivate HEV in a pig bioassay study **[quoted]** — [Barnaud et al., Appl Environ Microbiol 2012 (HAL record)](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02644150); a cell-culture study found inactivation at core 70°C for 2 minutes — [Swiss FSVO HEV risk profile](https://www.blv.admin.ch/dam/blv/de/dokumente/lebensmittel-und-ernaehrung/lebensmittelsicherheit/krankheitserreger-und-hygiene/riskprofile-hev.pdf.download.pdf/RiskProfile%20Hepatitis%20E%20Virus%20(HEV).pdf); a 2025 narrative review proposes 95°C for 10 minutes as one of the safest options for contaminated foods and notes chlorine results "are varied" — [Thermal Inactivation of Hepatitis E Virus: A Narrative Review (PubMed 40431713)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40431713/)

### Inferences
- Practical Bangladesh translation (synthesis, not a single quoted guideline):
  - Water: bring all drinking water (and water for infant feeds, for washing the baby's bottles, for mouth-rinsing) to a full rolling boil and keep it boiling ≥1 minute; store covered in a clean narrow-mouth container. Many urban household filters do not reliably remove viruses (not sourced here), so boiling is the safer default while the source is suspect.
  - Hands: soap and running water after toilet, after cleaning the patient's vomit/stool, after every diaper change, before cooking, before eating, before feeding the baby and before breastfeeding/handling the breast pump. The patient should wash hands before touching the baby; alcohol hand rub is less reliable than soap and water for HAV (non-enveloped virus; not sourced this session).
  - Food: patient does not cook or serve food for at least 1 week after jaundice onset if HAV (extend to ~3–4 weeks if HEV or cause unknown, matching WHO's HEV shedding window); eat freshly cooked hot food; avoid raw salads/cut fruit/street juices.
  - Toilet: if possible a separate toilet for the patient; otherwise wipe seat, flush handle, bodna/tap, door handle and basin daily with bleach. Typical household bleach is ~5% sodium hypochlorite, so 1:100 ≈ 10 mL bleach per 1 litre water (≈500 ppm) for routine surfaces; 1:10 (≈5,000 ppm) for visible stool/vomit contamination; 1-minute contact; make fresh daily; never mix with acid toilet cleaners (e.g., Harpic). (Arithmetic and local product strength are my inference; check the label concentration.)
  - Diapers: dispose of soiled diapers in a sealed bag; clean the changing surface with bleach solution; wash hands with soap afterwards; do not rinse cloth nappies in the kitchen basin.
  - Laundry/utensils: separate towel, plate, glass and spoon for the patient; wash utensils with detergent and hot water; wash soiled clothing/bed linen separately and sun-dry. No retrieved source gave a specific laundry temperature.
- The patient does not need to be isolated from the room or stop holding the baby; transmission is faecal-oral, not respiratory (inference from transmission route; hand hygiene before contact is the control).

### Gaps
- WHO/CDC primary pages on handwashing "key times" were not opened; hand-hygiene wording comes from US state health departments.
- No authoritative laundry temperature/duration for HAV or HEV was found.
- No HEV-specific surface disinfection guideline was found; the HAV bleach guidance is applied to HEV by analogy.
- **Conflicting/suspect extract on boiling and HEV:** one search summary stated that pork livers "boiled in water for 5 minutes were found to contain infective HEV". My recollection of the underlying study (Feagins et al. 2008) is the opposite — boiling or stir-frying for 5 minutes inactivated HEV, while 56°C for 1 hour did not. I could not open the paper to resolve this; do not print the "boiling for 5 minutes is insufficient" claim without checking [PMC3416424](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/3416424). This concerns solid tissue, not drinking water; WHO's rolling-boil guidance for water stands.
- Effectiveness of chlorine tablets/household chlorination against HEV: "varied" results; no CT values retrieved.

## 8. Bangladesh epidemiology: which virus, adult HAV immunity, seasonality

### Takeaway
In Bangladeshi adults with acute jaundice, HEV is the leading viral cause in most hospital series, while HAV is predominantly a childhood infection and nearly all adults over 30 were already immune in the one age-stratified community serosurvey (2009) — although urban, higher-income adolescents and adults are increasingly susceptible. Hence an adult father's acute jaundice is more likely HEV than HAV, but only serology can tell, and HBV must also be excluded.

### Cited Findings
- Tertiary hospital series, 144 acute viral hepatitis patients: HEV 58.33% (n=84), HBV 31.25% (n=45), HAV 4.86% (n=7) **[summary]** — ["HEV Infection as an Aetiologic Factor for Acute Hepatitis: Experience from a Tertiary Hospital in Bangladesh" (PMC2761807)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2761807/)
- Acute jaundice surveillance in six tertiary hospitals, 2014–2017: HEV caused 34% of acute jaundice in adults; HEV case fatality 5% overall, 12% in pregnant women; cases year-round **[summary]** — [Paul et al., bioRxiv 2019](https://biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2019/07/02/688721.full-text.pdf)
- Nationwide hospital-based study 2014–2015 (all ages): of 998 suspected acute hepatitis patients, 19% (191/998) anti-HAV IgM positive and 10% (103/998) anti-HEV IgM positive; HAV highest in children, HEV most prevalent in the 15–60-year group (12.4%) **[summary]** — [Khan et al., Annals of Global Health 2020 (PMC7082825)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7082825/); [journal page](https://annalsofglobalhealth.org/articles/2574)
- Community anti-HAV seroprevalence (published 2009): overall 69.6%, rising from 40.4% at 1–5 years to 98.4% at >30 years; lowest in high-SES groups; "the transition from high to intermediate HAV endemicity may be underway; high SES adolescents and adults remain particularly at risk of symptomatic illness" **[quoted/summary]** — [Saha et al., World J Gastroenterol 2009;15(39):4932](https://wjgnet.com/1007-9327/full/v15/i39/4932.htm)
- 2026 commentary: declining childhood exposure and increasing susceptibility among adolescents and adults, especially urban and higher-SES; calls for age-stratified seroprevalence and cost-effectiveness data **[summary]** — [BSMMUJ, "Time to reconsider hepatitis A vaccination policy in Bangladesh"](https://www.banglajol.info/index.php/BSMMUJ/article/download/87270/57581/245512?inline=1)
- Seasonality/outbreaks: Bangladeshi HEV outbreaks reviewed were all due to faecally contaminated water supply in the rainy season or hot summer months — [Viruses 2023;15(1):63](https://mdpi.com/1999-4915/15/1/63/html); hospital surveillance nonetheless found cases throughout the year — [Paul et al.](https://biorxiv.org/content/biorxiv/early/2019/07/02/688721.full-text.pdf); large urban Dhaka outbreak 2008–2009 — [Gurley et al., CID 2014](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/5/658/2895602) **[summaries]**
- Community epidemiology of HEV in urban Dhaka (study exists; details not retrieved) — [PMC5736947](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5736947/)

### Inferences
- For a Bangladeshi adult man with acute jaundice, prior probability favours HEV over HAV (HEV 34–58% of adult acute jaundice/hepatitis in hospital series vs HAV ~5% in the one adult series; ~98% of adults >30 HAV-immune in 2009). This is hospital-based data and may over-represent severe cases; HBV is the second most common cause in the older series and has completely different household implications (blood/sexual transmission; infant hepatitis B vaccination status matters).
- A Bangladeshi wife is probably already immune to HAV (if raised in typical conditions), reducing the need for HAV PEP; a wife from an affluent urban background is the exception.
- Because HEV immunity after childhood is far from universal in adults (HEV peaks at 15–60 years), the wife should be assumed susceptible to HEV.
- November falls after the monsoon; cases occur year-round, so season does not exclude HEV.

### Gaps
- No recent (post-2010) nationally representative age-specific anti-HAV IgG survey of Bangladeshi adults was found; the 98.4% figure is ~17 years old and measured total antibody.
- No adult-only HAV IgM proportion was retrieved from the 2014–2015 nationwide study.
- No IEDCR/DGHS outbreak bulletin for 2024–2026 was found (searches for the 2018 Chattogram/Halishahar outbreak returned nothing citable).
- Adult anti-HEV seroprevalence figures for Bangladesh were not retrieved.
