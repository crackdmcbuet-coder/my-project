# Hepatitis B and C in the father/husband: protecting the infant (<12 months) and the wife in Bangladesh

> Research date: 22 November 2026. **Method caveat:** in this session direct page fetching was blocked (cdc.gov, who.int, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, data.unicef.org did not resolve), so every finding below comes from web-search result extracts of the cited pages, not from reading the full page. Quotations are as returned by the search tool; wording marked "exact" should still be re-checked against the URL before being printed as a verbatim quote. Where a primary page (CDC/WHO) could not be read, a secondary page that reproduces it is cited and labelled as such.

## 1. HBV: transmission routes, survival on surfaces, incubation, infectivity, and what does NOT spread it

### Takeaway
HBV spreads through blood, semen and other body fluids (sex, shared razors/toothbrushes, contact with blood or open sores, mother-to-child); it stays infectious on surfaces for at least 7 days. It is not spread by food, water, shared utensils, hugging, kissing, coughing, sneezing or breastfeeding, so ordinary family life with the patient is safe as long as blood exposure is avoided and contacts are vaccinated.

### Cited Findings
- CDC states HBV "can survive outside the body and remains infectious for at least 7 days" — [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis B](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)
- CDC lists transmission through blood, semen and other body fluids, including "sharing personal items, such as toothbrushes or razors" and "direct contact with the blood or open sores of a person who has hepatitis B" — [CDC Hepatitis B Prevention and Control](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/prevention/)
- OSHA, attributing to CDC: HBV "can survive for at least one week in dried blood on environmental surfaces or contaminated needles and instruments" — [OSHA Hospitals eTool](https://www.osha.gov/etools/hospitals/housekeeping/biological-hazards)
- What does not spread HBV (Maine CDC fact sheet reproducing US CDC wording): "Hepatitis B is not spread through food or water, sharing eating utensils, breastfeeding, hugging, kissing, coughing, sneezing, or by casual contact" — [Maine DHHS HBV fact sheet](https://www.maine.gov/dhhs/mecdc/infectious-disease/epi/shm/HBV_FS.pdf); same statement on the CDC page — [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis B](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hbv/HBVfaq.htm)
- Incubation (CDC): abnormal liver enzymes appear on average about 60 days after exposure; symptoms appear on average about 90 days after exposure, range 60–150 days — [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis B](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html)
- People without symptoms can still transmit: "Many people with hepatitis B have no symptoms, but these people can still spread the virus" — [CDC Hepatitis B Prevention and Control](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/prevention/)
- Risk of chronic infection depends on age at infection: "Approximately 90% of infected infants and 30% of children infected between 1–5 years will remain chronically infected"; about 95% of infected adults recover completely — [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis B](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hbv/HBVfaq.htm). HHS gives 2–6% of adults becoming chronic — [HHS Hepatitis B Basics](https://www.hhs.gov/hepatitis/learn-about-viral-hepatitis/hepatitis-b-basics/index.html)
- Household clustering in Bangladesh: a Dhaka (Kamalapur) community study found much higher HBV prevalence among family members of HBsAg-positive people than in the general community — [Ashraf et al., BMC Infect Dis 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2918606)
- Breastfeeding by an HBV-positive mother: CDC says mothers with hepatitis B or C can breastfeed; HBV transmission risk through breastfeeding is "negligible" if the infant is vaccinated; no need to delay breastfeeding until the infant is fully immunized; stop temporarily if nipples are cracked or bleeding — [CDC Hepatitis B or C Infections and Breastfeeding](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/illnesses-conditions/hepatitis-b-c.html)
- WHO hepatitis B fact sheet (dated 23 July 2025): vaccine "offers nearly 100% protection"; WHO estimates 254 million people living with hepatitis B — [WHO Hepatitis B fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-b)

### Inferences
- The infant is the family member with the most to lose: an adult who catches HBV clears it ~95% of the time, an infant becomes a lifelong carrier ~90% of the time. That asymmetry is why infant protection (vaccine ± HBIG) is the priority.
- The 7-day surface survival means dried blood (razor, nail cutter, toothbrush, blood-stained cloth, shaving nicks) is the realistic household hazard, not shared meals or cuddling.
- Kissing is on CDC's "not spread by" list; the residual theoretical concern is visible blood (bleeding gums, mouth sores, cracked lips) contacting the baby's mouth or broken skin.

### Gaps
- The often-quoted "HBV is 50–100 times more infectious than HIV" could not be confirmed from a primary source in this session.
- WHO's own wording on incubation (commonly cited as 30–180 days) and on 7-day survival could not be retrieved; only the CDC figures above were confirmed.
- No source retrieved that quantifies per-act or per-year risk to a spouse or a baby from an HBsAg-positive household member, or how infectivity differs by HBeAg/viral load.

## 2. HBV post-exposure prophylaxis and vaccination for the wife (sexual partner): acute vs chronic HBV in the husband

### Takeaway
For a susceptible sexual partner of someone with ACUTE hepatitis B, ACIP guidance is one dose of HBIG 0.06 mL/kg plus starting the hepatitis B vaccine series, provided it can begin within 14 days of the last sexual contact. For CHRONIC HBV, contacts are tested and the susceptible ones vaccinated, with condoms until immunity is confirmed and an anti-HBs check 1–2 months after the last dose.

### Cited Findings
- Acute HBV, sexual partner: susceptible sexual partners "should receive a single dose of HBIG (0.06 mL/kg) and should begin the hepatitis B vaccine series if prophylaxis can be started within 14 days of the last sexual contact" (ACIP; older document, dose and window unchanged in later monographs) — [ACIP, Protection Against Viral Hepatitis, MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00041917.htm); same regimen (HBIG with first vaccine dose within 14 days of most recent contact) — [Drugs.com HBIG monograph](https://www.drugs.com/monograph/hepatitis-b-immune-globulin.html)
- Testing the partner first is acceptable only if it does not delay HBIG beyond 14 days — [ACIP Postexposure Prophylaxis of Hepatitis B, MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00022736.htm)
- CDC STI Treatment Guidelines 2021: vaccine series plus HBIG is indicated for unvaccinated people after percutaneous/mucosal exposure to HBsAg-positive blood or sex/needle-sharing contact with an HBsAg-positive person; an unfinished series should be completed; a previously vaccinated person never tested after vaccination gets a single booster dose — [CDC STI Treatment Guidelines 2021](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/rr/rr7004a1.htm)
- STI Guidelines 2021: HBIG alone works, but because vaccine is available HBIG is typically used together with vaccination — [CDC STI Treatment Guidelines 2021](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/rr/rr7004a1.htm)
- Chronic HBV: ACIP 2018 lists "household, sexual, or needle contacts of HBsAg-positive persons" among groups for serologic testing before vaccination — [ACIP 2018, MMWR 67(RR-1)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/rr/rr6701a1.htm)
- Post-vaccination testing: for sex partners of HBsAg-positive persons, anti-HBs testing "1–2 months after the final dose of vaccine is recommended"; protective level is anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL — [ACIP 2018, MMWR 67(RR-1) PDF](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/rr/pdfs/rr6701-H.PDF)
- If the index patient becomes chronically infected, all household contacts should be vaccinated — [ACIP, MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/00041917.htm)
- WHO 2024 chronic hepatitis B guidelines (as summarised by TREAT Asia): catch-up vaccination for "household and sexual contacts of people who have chronic HBV infection (according to a positive HBsAg test)"; screen all family members and sexual contacts with HBsAg and vaccinate those not yet vaccinated — [TREAT Asia fact sheet](https://aidsdatahub.org/sites/default/files/resource/ta-fact-sheet-101524v1147.pdf); guideline itself — [WHO 2024 HBV guidelines](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240090903)
- Barrier protection: a clinical reference table for HBsAg-positive persons recommends having household and sexual contacts vaccinated, using barrier protection during sex if the partner is not vaccinated or immune, not sharing toothbrushes/razors, covering cuts, and cleaning blood spills with bleach solution — [PMC5975958, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5975958/table/T5)
- Pregnancy/breastfeeding and the vaccine: "pregnancy is not a contraindication to hepatitis B vaccination" — [Institute for Vaccine Safety](https://vaccinesafety.edu/vaccine-preventable-diseases-3/); Engerix-B, Recombivax HB and Heplisav-B may all be used in pregnancy, no apparent risk to the fetus — [Immunize.org](https://www.immunize.org/ask-experts/is-it-safe-for-a-person-at-risk-of-hepatitis-b-to-be-vaccinated-during-pregnancy); breastfeeding is not a contraindication and does not interfere with the infant's response — [LactMed via Drugs.com](https://drugs.com/breastfeeding/hepatitis-b-vaccine.html)
- Adult dose: 20 mcg in 1 mL, 3-dose regimen — [ePharma Bangladesh product page](https://epharma.com.bd/en/medicines/vaccines/hepa-b-for-adult-vaccine-1ml-15552)

### Inferences
- The wife's 14-day clock runs from the LAST unprotected sexual contact, not from the day jaundice appeared; if the couple had sex in the fortnight before diagnosis she may still be inside the window, so this is time-critical.
- If she is past 14 days, or HBIG is unobtainable, the practical fallback is to test (HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc) and start vaccine immediately if susceptible; vaccine alone is still worthwhile because exposure is ongoing.
- If she is pregnant, her HBsAg result matters twice: for her own care and because an HBsAg-positive mother means the newborn needs birth-dose vaccine + HBIG, which the Bangladesh public schedule does not provide by default (see section 4).

### Gaps
- The exact ACIP 2018 PEP table wording (including the 7-day window for percutaneous exposure) could not be retrieved; the 0.06 mL/kg / 14-day statement is confirmed only from the older ACIP document and the Drugs.com monograph.
- The standard adult schedule of 0, 1 and 6 months was not confirmed from a source in this session (only "3 doses, 20 mcg" was); verify in [ACIP 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/rr/rr6701a1.htm).
- Adult seroprotection rate after 3 doses (commonly quoted ~90% in healthy adults under 40) was not confirmed.
- A search extract mentions "ACIP and CDC's December 2025 changes to their Hepatitis B vaccination recommendations" which some bodies dispute — [Institute for Vaccine Safety](https://vaccinesafety.edu/vaccine-preventable-diseases-3/). I could not read the text of those changes; US guidance may therefore differ from the 2018 document in ways not captured here.
- "Use condoms until anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL is documented" is the logical combination of the barrier and post-vaccination-testing recommendations above; I did not retrieve a single CDC sentence stating it in those words.

## 3. Infant under 12 months as a household contact of an acute HBV case

### Takeaway
Guidance derived from ACIP/AAP singles out infants under 12 months whose mother or primary caregiver has acute HBV: an unvaccinated infant gets HBIG (0.5 mL) plus the vaccine series; an infant already in the series completes it on schedule. Other household contacts do not need HBIG unless they had identifiable blood exposure, but all should be vaccinated.

### Cited Findings
- "Unvaccinated infants exposed to a primary caretaker with acute hepatitis B should receive HBIG (0.5 mL) and should initiate and complete the 3-dose hepatitis B vaccine series" — [Iowa HHS Epi Manual, Hepatitis B](https://hhs.iowa.gov/health-prevention/providers-professionals/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis-b-0)
- "Infants (ie, younger than 12 months of age) who have close contact with primary care-givers with acute infection and who have begun the immunization series should complete the series on schedule" — [Maryland Dept of Health, Perinatal Hepatitis B](https://health.maryland.gov/phpa/OIDEOR/IMMUN/Shared%20Documents/imm_hepbrpt.cfm.html)
- Drugs.com monograph (citing AAP/ACIP): for unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated infants <12 months exposed to a mother/primary caregiver with acute HBV, give HBIG and start or complete the primary HepB series — [Drugs.com HBIG monograph](https://www.drugs.com/monograph/hepatitis-b-immune-globulin.html)
- Irish national guidance matches: HBIG and vaccine for unimmunised infants <12 months if mother or primary caregiver has acute HBV — [NIAC Immunisation Guidelines Ch. 9](https://www.hiqa.ie/sites/default/files/NIAC/Immunisation_Guidelines/Chapter_09_Hepatitis_B.pdf)
- Other household contacts: HBIG "is not indicated unless they have identifiable blood exposure to the index patient, such as by sharing of toothbrushes or razors" — [Maryland Dept of Health](https://health.maryland.gov/phpa/OIDEOR/IMMUN/Shared%20Documents/imm_hepbrpt.cfm.html); same in the FDA label — [Nabi-HB label, FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/74707/download)
- 3-dose infant series: about 95% of healthy infants reach protective anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL — [CDC ACIP briefing, Sept 2025](https://cdc.gov/acip/downloads/slides-2025-09-18-19/hep-b-birth-dose-briefing-508.pdf); "Approximately 95% of immunocompetent infants vaccinated with 3 doses" — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6150009)
- In infants of HBsAg-positive mothers, 1.7% (21/1255) had anti-HBs <10 mIU/mL after vaccination — [PMC study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8903900)
- Bangladesh field evidence that the 6-10-14-week schedule without birth dose controls childhood infection: child HBsAg prevalence about 0.05% in the 2011–2012 serosurvey; regional ≤1% target verified in 2019 — [CDC MMWR 2020, SEAR hepatitis B control](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6930a2.htm); [icddr,b news](https://www.icddrb.org/news-and-events/news?id=847)

### Inferences
- The guidance is written for the mother/primary caregiver. A father with acute HBV who shares hands-on care (feeding, bathing, sleeping beside the baby) fits the intent, but whether he counts as "primary caregiver" is a clinical judgement for the paediatrician, not something the texts settle.
- In Bangladesh the first HepB-containing dose is at 6 weeks, so a baby under 6 weeks has no protection at all and a baby between 6 and 14 weeks is only partly protected. These are the infants for whom HBIG plus an immediate (early) vaccine dose matters most.
- A baby who has completed Penta 1-2-3 is very likely (~95%) protected and, by the guidance above, needs no HBIG.
- The guidance addresses ACUTE infection in the caregiver. For a chronic carrier father the route is simply "ensure the infant's series is complete and on time"; no source retrieved recommends HBIG in that situation.

### Gaps
- The current AAP Red Book wording could not be retrieved. The two secondary sources differ on the partially vaccinated infant: Maryland says "complete the series on schedule" (no HBIG), Drugs.com groups "incompletely vaccinated" with unvaccinated infants for HBIG. Unverified recollection of older Red Book editions (not retrieved, do not quote): infant with ≥2 doses presumed protected; with 1 dose give the 2nd dose if due or HBIG if not due; with 0 doses give HBIG 0.5 mL + vaccine. Needs checking.
- No explicit statement found for "fully vaccinated infant needs nothing"; it follows from the texts but is not quoted.
- No guidance found on giving an early monovalent HepB dose before 6 weeks to a Bangladeshi infant exposed in the household (WHO supports doses from birth, but a specific exposure-driven recommendation was not located).

## 4. Bangladesh EPI schedule, hepatitis B birth dose status, coverage, and 2026 supply problems

### Takeaway
Bangladesh gives hepatitis B only inside the pentavalent vaccine at 6, 10 and 14 weeks; a universal birth dose had not been adopted per the latest sources found (WHO 2024 data, 2023 coverage 0%). Coverage is historically high, but central stock-outs of pentavalent and other vaccines were reported in April–May 2026, so individual infants may be behind schedule.

### Cited Findings
- HepB vaccine was introduced into the EPI in 2003–2005 using the 6, 10, 14 week schedule — [CDC MMWR 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6930a2.htm)
- Birth dose "was, however, not included in the child vaccination schedule in Bangladesh because of the difficulties to administer a timely birth dose" — [icddr,b](https://www.icddrb.org/news-and-events/news?id=847)
- A 2021 review lists Bangladesh and Nepal as the regional countries that had not adopted HepB birth dose — [Vaccines 2021, MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-393X/9/4/374/htm)
- Latest status found: universal HepB birth dose policy within 24 h "Not Adopted" (citing WHO, 2024); birth-dose coverage 0% (2023) — [Coalition for Global Hepatitis Elimination, Bangladesh profile](https://www.globalhep.org/data-profiles/countries/bangladesh)
- WHO position: all babies should get hepatitis B vaccine as soon as possible after birth (within 24 hours), followed by two or three doses at least four weeks apart — [WHO Hepatitis B fact sheet, 23 July 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-b)
- Schedule as described in Bangladeshi sources: BCG at birth; pentavalent (DPT-HepB-Hib), OPV and PCV at 6, 10, 14 weeks; injectable polio at 6 and 14 weeks; MR at 9 and 15 months; TCV one dose at 9 months; vaccines against 10 diseases from birth to 15 months — [Jago News](https://www.jagonews24.com/en/national/news/91363)
- IPV is given as fractional IPV (fIPV) at 6 and 14 weeks — [DoctorsFind BD EPI guide (secondary)](https://www.doctorsfindbd.com/en/blog/child-vaccination-schedule-epi-bangladesh); the EPI coverage survey counts "two doses of IPV" alongside three Penta, three OPV, three PCV and MR — [EPI CES 2023](https://www.sdg.gov.bd/uploads/indicator-data/EPI%20CES%202023%20-%20First%2028%20pages.pdf)
- TCV: nationwide campaign launched 12 October 2025 for ~50 million children aged 9 months to <15 years; routine use for under-one-year children planned from 2026 — [UNICEF Bangladesh](https://www.unicef.org/bangladesh/en/press-releases/bangladesh-launches-nationwide-typhoid-conjugate-vaccine-campaign-protect-50-million). Campaign ran 13 Oct–13 Nov 2025 and vaccinated over 42 million — [WHO SEARO](https://www.who.int/southeastasia/news-room/item/25-02-2026-tcv-introduction-in-bangladesh-october-november-2025); DGHS figure 43.2 million — [Gavi](https://www.gavi.org/news/media-room/bangladesh-launches-nationwide-typhoid-conjugate-vaccine-campaign-protect-50)
- HPV: single-dose HPV vaccine introduced since 2023 for girls aged 10–14 — [UNICEF Bangladesh](https://www.unicef.org/bangladesh/en/press-releases/nearly-half-million-children-bangladesh-miss-full-immunization)
- Rotavirus: not in the national schedule; Bangladesh and Sri Lanka were the South Asian exceptions, with hopes pinned on a January 2026 Gavi application round — [Country Learning Hub Bangladesh](https://clh-immunisation-bd.org/features/bangladeshs-next-immunisation-milestone-stakeholders-unite-to-add-rotavirus-vaccine-to-bangladeshs-epi-schedule/)
- Coverage: valid full vaccination coverage 81.6% in the 2023 Coverage Evaluation Survey — [UNICEF/WHO/Gavi release](https://www.unicef.org/bangladesh/en/press-releases/nearly-half-million-children-bangladesh-miss-full-immunization)
- HepB3 coverage 97% for 2024, as reported by a data aggregator citing WHO (not verified against WUENIC) — [TheGlobalEconomy](https://www.theglobaleconomy.com/Bangladesh/hepatitis_B_immunization_rate/)
- WUENIC notes flag Bangladesh as having a 5–10 point gap between reported and estimated coverage — [WUENIC 2024 revision notes](https://data.unicef.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/WUENIC_notes_for_AllCountries_2024rev.pdf); administrative DTP1 exceeded 100% throughout 2019–2023, indicating data-quality problems — [Gavi Zero-Dose Learning Hub](https://zdlh.gavi.org/sites/default/files/2024-12/Bangladesh_ZDLH_SAR_Data_Dashboard_Jan-June_2024.pdf)
- 2026 stock-outs: central stock of pentavalent reported as zero (monthly demand 10 million doses) — [bdnews24, May 2026](https://bdnews24.com/health/863aa4767a72); six vaccines (BCG, pentavalent, bOPV, PCV, MR, Td) at zero in the central warehouse with field-level shortages — [Prothom Alo](https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/yqni6rdl84)

### Inferences
- Because there is no birth dose, every Bangladeshi infant is unprotected against HBV for the first 6 weeks and not fully protected until after 14 weeks. In a household with an infectious adult, that gap is the main vulnerability.
- Given the 2026 stock-outs, the family should check the baby's EPI card for actual Penta 1/2/3 dates instead of assuming doses were given on time; private-market paediatric HepB vaccine exists (section 6) if the public supply is interrupted.

### Gaps
- No official DGHS/EPI schedule document was retrieved; the schedule above is from news and secondary sites. One Scribd table lists a monovalent HepB birth dose and rotavirus in the schedule, which conflicts with every other source and looks like a private/paediatric-association schedule — [Scribd](https://www.scribd.com/document/1005676857/EPI-Schedule-Table-Bangladesh).
- Whether a birth dose was introduced, or rotavirus approved, during 2026 could not be confirmed; no announcement was found. The latest dated evidence for "no birth dose" is WHO 2024 data.
- The authoritative WUENIC HepB3/DTP3 figures (2024 and the July 2026 release for 2025) were not readable — [WUENIC Bangladesh](https://data.unicef.org/wp-content/uploads/cp/immunisation/wuenic_bgd.pdf), [Gavi country page](https://www.gavi.org/country/bangladesh).
- Whether routine infant TCV at 9 months actually started in 2026, and whether the 2026 stock-outs were resolved by November 2026, is unknown.

## 5. Bangladesh HBV and HCV prevalence

### Takeaway
HBV is of low-intermediate endemicity in Bangladeshi adults (pooled ~4%, individual community studies roughly 5.5–6.5%) and now very rare in vaccinated children (~0.05%); HCV is uncommon in the general population (under 1%).

### Cited Findings
- Pooled general-population HBV prevalence 4.0% (95% CI 3.0–5.1) across 15 studies from 1995–2017, with high heterogeneity (I² = 96%) — [meta-analysis, PMC8895722](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8895722)
- Kamalapur, Dhaka community study (2005–2006): extract reads "14 (0.7%) were positive for HBsAg, 452 (22.6%) for anti-HBc and 116 (5.8%) for both"; anti-HCV 0.2% (4 of 1,997), another 0.3% positive for both anti-HBc and anti-HCV; authors classed HBV as intermediate endemicity and HCV as rare — [Ashraf et al., BMC Infect Dis 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2918606). Reading note: the categories appear mutually exclusive, so total HBsAg positivity would be about 6.5% (0.7% + 5.8%), not 0.7%; confirm against the paper before quoting a single figure.
- Earlier Savar (semi-urban Dhaka) estimate: HBsAg 5.5% — [cited in Ashraf et al.](https://bmcinfectdis.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1471-2334-10-208)
- Anti-HCV 0.88% among 1,018 people in a semi-urban area near Dhaka — [BSMMU Journal 2009](https://doaj.org/article/0006532950274b639ebdfddbaccaf008)
- General Bangladeshi HCV prevalence described as under 1% — [PMC6969329](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6969329)
- A 2018 review judged conclusive national HBV estimates difficult beyond a declining trend — [PMC6265756](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6265756)
- Children after vaccine introduction: HBsAg about 0.05% (2011–2012 serosurvey) — [CDC MMWR 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6930a2.htm)
- Causes of adult acute jaundice in Bangladesh: in a multicentre hospital surveillance study of patients aged ≥14 years, 34% (661) had acute hepatitis E and 8% (48) hepatitis A — [PLoS NTD 2020](https://doaj.org/article/518f3e6ca26c4f2cb7f8e89839865bb7); in children HAV is the commonest cause, then HEV — [MedCrave paediatric series](https://medcraveonline.com/GHOA/clinicopathological-profile-of-acute-hepatitis-in-children-a-tertiary-center-experience-from-bangladesh.html)

### Inferences
- With roughly 1 in 25 adults HBsAg-positive, an adult with jaundice plus a positive HBsAg may be a long-standing carrier with a flare or with superimposed hepatitis E/A, not necessarily new acute hepatitis B; IgM anti-HBc and HEV/HAV IgM are needed to tell these apart (section 9).
- HCV is an unlikely cause of acute jaundice in this setting, both because prevalence is low and because acute HCV is rarely icteric; a positive anti-HCV is more likely an incidental chronic infection.

### Gaps
- No nationally representative adult serosurvey was found. A national biomarker survey of HBsAg and anti-HCV in children 2–5 and pregnant women was planned for May 2024–March 2025, results not found — [WHO SEARO research portal](https://researchportal.searo.who.int/index.php/hrp/search/viewProposal/163).
- The mapping of the meta-analysis and 2018 review to the two PMC URLs above was inferred from the search results and should be checked.
- The share of adult acute jaundice due to HBV in the surveillance study was not captured.

## 6. Availability and cost of hepatitis B vaccine and HBIG in Bangladesh

### Takeaway
Adult and paediatric hepatitis B vaccines are sold in Bangladeshi pharmacies at a few hundred taka per dose, including a locally made brand; HBIG is listed as available but costs roughly ten times more per vial and its stocking is uncertain.

### Cited Findings
- Hepa-B (Incepta, locally manufactured) adult 1 mL: MRP ৳550, listed around ৳517–523 — [ePharma](https://epharma.com.bd/en/medicines/vaccines/hepa-b-for-adult-vaccine-1ml-15552)
- Hepa-B paediatric 10 mcg/0.5 mL is listed — [Arogga](https://www.arogga.com/product/9040/hepa-b-for-pediatric-injection-10mcg-0-5ml)
- Engerix-B (GSK) 10 mcg/0.5 mL: MRP ৳386, about ৳378 discounted; several listings showed it out of stock — [ePharma](https://epharma.com.bd/en/medicines/vaccines/engerix-b-05-3862); 20 mcg product listed — [Arogga](https://www.arogga.com/product/23236/engerix-b-injection-20mcg)
- Hepa-B was produced through technology transfer and tested in a non-inferiority trial against the imported vaccine — [trial registry summary](https://ctv.veeva.com/study/non-inferiority-trial-of-locally-manufactured-hepa-b-vaccine-in-bangladesh)
- HBIG: "Immunohbs" 180 IU/mL injection (ZAS Corporation) listed as available in Bangladesh at ৳5,430 — [DIMS](https://dimsbd.com/brand/immunohbs); ৳4,887 on another listing — [Arogga](https://www.arogga.com/product/40750/immuno-hbs-injection-180iu-ml)
- HBIG potency and contents vary between countries and manufacturers — [Arogga product note](https://www.arogga.com/product/40750/immuno-hbs-injection-180iu-ml)

### Inferences
- A full 3-dose adult course for the wife costs on the order of ৳1,200–1,700 for the vaccine itself at these list prices (3 × ৳386–550), excluding administration fees.
- The US dose "0.06 mL/kg" is written for US-licensed HBIG; a product labelled in IU/mL at a different potency cannot be dosed by that volume rule without checking the insert, so the prescriber must convert.
- HBIG's price and patchy stocking make it realistic mainly for the highest-value uses (wife within 14 days of last sexual contact with an acute case; unvaccinated infant).

### Gaps
- Online pharmacy prices are retail listings on the day of search, not verified current stock; no government-facility price or free-supply information was found.
- No source found on whether public hospitals stock HBIG, or on cold-chain reliability of private-market vaccine.

## 7. HCV: transmission, risk to wife and infant, testing, treatment

### Takeaway
HCV is spread by blood; sexual transmission in long-term monogamous heterosexual couples is very rare (maximum estimate 0.07% per year), household spread is uncommon, and it is not spread by breastfeeding, food or casual contact. There is no vaccine, but treatment cures over 95% of people in about 8–12 weeks.

### Cited Findings
- "Hepatitis C is not spread by sharing eating utensils, breastfeeding, hugging, kissing, holding hands, coughing, or sneezing. It is also not spread through food or water" — [CDC Hepatitis C Q&A for the Public](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcv/cfaq.htm)
- Breastfeeding: no evidence it spreads HCV; women with cracked or bleeding nipples should stop nursing temporarily until healed — [CDC Hepatitis C Q&A](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcv/cfaq.htm)
- Sexual transmission risk "is believed to be low"; condoms are still advised — [CDC Hepatitis C Prevention and Control](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-c/prevention)
- HCV Partners Study (Terrault et al., Hepatology 2013): 500 anti-HCV-positive, HIV-negative people and their long-term monogamous heterosexual partners; 20 partners (4%) were anti-HCV-positive but only 3 couples (0.6%) had viral strains related enough to indicate transmission within the couple; maximum incidence 0.07% per year (about 1 per 190,000 sexual contacts) over 8,377 person-years; no specific sexual practice was linked to infection — [PMC4384338](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4384338); [aidsmap summary](https://www.aidsmap.com/news/mar-2013/sexual-transmission-hepatitis-c-very-rare-monogamous-heterosexual-couples)
- A later review by the same group gives 7.2 per 10,000 person-years as the maximal incidence and 1 per 380,000 contacts in one estimate, and warns the results do not apply to all populations — [Dodge & Terrault, PMC4659344](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4659344)
- Household transmission "does not occur very often"; when it does it is most likely through direct through-the-skin exposure to an infected household member's blood — [CDC Hepatitis C Q&A for Health Professionals](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/HCV/HCVfaq.htm)
- CDC advises not sharing razors, toothbrushes, nail clippers or other personal-care items that may carry traces of blood — [CDC Hepatitis C Q&A for Health Professionals](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/HCV/HCVfaq.htm)
- HCV RNA was found on a considerable share of toothbrushes used by hepatitis C patients — [PubMed 16907842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16907842/)
- Survival outside the body: up to 6 weeks on surfaces at room temperature in a Yale study — [HIV.gov](https://www.hiv.gov/blog/scientists-discover-hepatitis-c-virus-can-remain-infectious-outside-of-the-body-for-up-to-6-weeks); up to 3 weeks per another review — [Hepatitis C Online, Univ. of Washington](https://www.hepatitisc.uw.edu/pdf/screening-diagnosis/counseling-prevention/core-concept/all)
- No vaccine: "There is currently no effective vaccine against hepatitis C" — [WHO Hepatitis C fact sheet, 25 July 2025](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-c)
- Cure: direct-acting antivirals "can cure more than 95% of persons with hepatitis C infection" — [WHO Hepatitis C fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-c); treatments "can cure most people with hepatitis C in 8–12 weeks" — [CDC Hepatitis C Basics](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-c/about)
- Mother-to-child and sexual routes exist but "are less common" — [WHO Hepatitis C fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-c)

### Inferences
- If the husband has HCV, the wife's realistic risk comes from shared blood-contaminated items and, far less, from sex; a baseline anti-HCV test for her (with HCV RNA if positive) is reasonable reassurance, not an emergency.
- The infant's risk from an HCV-positive father is essentially limited to blood contact; routine testing of the baby is not suggested by any source found unless the mother is infected.
- Curing the husband with DAAs removes the source, which is the only "prophylaxis" available to the family.

### Gaps
- An explicit CDC statement that there is no post-exposure prophylaxis for HCV (no immunoglobulin, no prophylactic antivirals) was not retrieved; verify at [CDC HCV FAQ for professionals](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/HCV/HCVfaq.htm).
- Timing of tests after exposure (HCV RNA detectable from ~1–2 weeks, anti-HCV later) was not confirmed from a source.
- DAA availability and price in Bangladesh were not researched.
- Household non-sexual transmission risk is described as hard to quantify; no numeric estimate was found — [Hepatitis C Online](https://www.hepatitisc.uw.edu/pdf/screening-diagnosis/counseling-prevention/core-concept/all).

## 8. Practical household precautions for blood-borne hepatitis (HBV/HCV), including handling the baby

### Takeaway
Precautions centre on blood: no shared razors, toothbrushes or nail clippers, cover cuts, clean blood with diluted bleach, use condoms until the partner is immune, and do not pre-chew the baby's food. Hugging, holding, sharing meals and normal kissing are not transmission routes.

### Cited Findings
- Recommendations for HBsAg-positive people: have household and sexual contacts vaccinated; use barrier protection during sex if the partner is not vaccinated or immune; do not share toothbrushes, razors, injection equipment or glucose meters; cover cuts; clean blood spills with bleach solution — [PMC5975958, Table 5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5975958/table/T5)
- Cover cuts and open sores; do not share personal items — [Rhode Island Dept of Health](https://health.ri.gov/hepatitis-viral/hepatitis-b); household members should be tested and vaccinated — [CDC Hepatitis B Prevention and Control](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/prevention/)
- Bleach dilution: disinfect blood with "ten parts water to one part bleach" — [University of Kentucky EHS fact sheet](https://ehs.uky.edu/fact-sheets/cleaning-blood-and-body-fluids); blood spills including dried blood cleaned with one part household bleach to 10 parts water — [Alabama Public Health, Hepatitis C](https://www.alabamapublichealth.gov/hepatitis/hep-c.html)
- CDC's own spill job aid specifies an EPA-registered disinfectant effective against HBV, HIV and other bloodborne pathogens — [CDC blood/body fluid job aid](https://reach.cdc.gov/sites/default/files/job-aids-resources/Blood_and_Body_Fluid_Exposure_508_0.pdf)
- Premastication: "Transmission of hepatitis B virus and group A streptococcus by premastication has been documented"; CDC recommends HIV-infected caregivers not pre-chew food for children and that the public be educated on the risk — [CDC MMWR 2011;60(9)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6009a2.htm). Reported under the headline that caregivers with infectious diseases should avoid pre-chewing food for babies — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20120331/cdc-caregivers-with-infectious-diseases-should-avoid-prechewing-food-for-babies)
- Mechanism: transmission by pre-chewed food is presumed to need blood in the caregiver's mouth; in two of three HIV cases the caregiver had bleeding gums — [CDC MMWR 2011;60(9)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6009a2.htm)
- Kissing and sharing utensils are not transmission routes for HBV — [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis B](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hbv/HBVfaq.htm); nor for HCV — [CDC Hepatitis C Q&A](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis/hcv/cfaq.htm)

### Inferences
- The formal CDC premastication recommendation is written for HIV; applying it to an HBV-positive father is an extension, though a well-founded one since HBV transmission by this route is documented in the same report.
- Items worth naming for a Bangladeshi household: shared razor/blade (including barbershop shaves while infectious), toothbrush or miswak, nail cutter, ear/nose-piercing tools, and any blood-stained cloth; the father should not bite the baby's nails or touch the baby with uncovered bleeding cuts.
- Feeding the baby by hand, sharing a plate, carrying, bathing and sleeping beside the baby carry no recognised risk in the absence of blood.

### Gaps
- No CDC page was retrieved that states the 1:10 bleach dilution for home use in its own words; the ratio comes from a university and a state health department.
- No guideline text found specifically on a hepatitis-positive father's contact with an infant (all infant guidance is framed around mothers/primary caregivers).

## 9. Which blood tests distinguish the causes of acute jaundice

### Takeaway
The standard acute viral hepatitis work-up is IgM anti-HAV, HBsAg with IgM anti-HBc, anti-HCV (with HCV RNA to confirm), and IgM anti-HEV. In Bangladeshi adults hepatitis E is the most frequent finding, so a positive HBsAg alone does not prove hepatitis B caused the jaundice.

### Cited Findings
- Acute hepatitis A and E are diagnosed by positive IgM anti-HAV and IgM anti-HEV respectively; acute hepatitis B by positive HBsAg plus IgM anti-HBc — [Bangladesh Medical Journal, BanglaJOL](https://banglajol.info/index.php/BMJ/article/view/33127/22354)
- IgM anti-HEV assays vary in sensitivity, specificity and agreement between commercial kits — [CDC Stacks document 154381](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/154381)
- Co-infection is real: half of patients who died with acute hepatitis E in a Bangladesh surveillance study were co-infected with HAV or HBV — [PLoS NTD 2020](https://doaj.org/article/518f3e6ca26c4f2cb7f8e89839865bb7)
- Hepatitis E was the leading identified cause (34%) among hospitalised jaundiced patients aged ≥14 in Bangladesh — [PLoS NTD 2020](https://doaj.org/article/518f3e6ca26c4f2cb7f8e89839865bb7)
- Tests for contacts of an HBsAg-positive person before vaccination: serologic testing is recommended for household, sexual and needle contacts — [ACIP 2018](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/rr/rr6701a1.htm); WHO-derived guidance uses HBsAg for family and sexual contacts — [TREAT Asia summary of WHO 2024](https://aidsdatahub.org/sites/default/files/resource/ta-fact-sheet-101524v1147.pdf)
- Immunity threshold after vaccination: anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL — [ACIP 2018 PDF](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/rr/pdfs/rr6701-H.PDF)

### Inferences
- What the family should ask for, for the husband: IgM anti-HAV, IgM anti-HEV, HBsAg, IgM anti-HBc, anti-HCV (and HCV RNA if anti-HCV is positive or suspicion is high). For the wife if he is HBsAg-positive: HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc. If he is anti-HCV/RNA positive: anti-HCV for her.
- HBsAg positive with IgM anti-HBc negative points to chronic HBV with another cause of jaundice (often HEV in Bangladesh) or a flare; this changes the wife's management from urgent HBIG-plus-vaccine to test-and-vaccinate. High-titre IgM anti-HBc can also appear in flares, so the clinician interprets it.
- Hepatitis A and E are spread by the faecal-oral route, with entirely different household precautions (water, food, handwashing) from HBV/HCV; identifying the virus determines which precautions the family needs.

### Gaps
- No guideline-level algorithm (window periods, anti-HBc total vs IgM interpretation table, repeat HBsAg at 6 months to define chronicity) was retrieved; the CDC serology interpretation table should be consulted at [CDC Clinical Overview of Hepatitis B](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html).
- Local availability and cost of IgM anti-HEV and HCV RNA testing in Bangladesh were not researched.
