## 1. Breastfeeding guidance by virus (mother exposed or infected): hepatitis A, E, B, C

### Takeaway
For hepatitis A, B and C the guidance found is consistent: breastfeeding continues (HAV: with strict handwashing and immune globulin for the infant where indicated; HBV: safe, infant should be vaccinated; HCV: safe, pause only from a cracked/bleeding nipple). Hepatitis E is the one real uncertainty: HEV RNA has been found in colostrum/milk, transmission through breastfeeding has not been documented, MSF and e-lactancia say continue, but at least one review advises against breastfeeding during *symptomatic* acute maternal HEV.

### Cited Findings

**Hepatitis A (HAV)**
- "Hepatitis A, even during the acute infectious period, is not a contraindication to breastfeeding"; "there is no evidence for transmission through breastmilk"; some experts have advised giving the infant immune globulin if the mother has onset of symptoms from two weeks before to one week after delivery; even without immune globulin, severe disease has not been reported in infants; handwashing by the mother is stressed. (Pediatric hospital parent/clinician handout, originally 2014.) — [Rady Children's Hospital San Diego – Breastfeeding and Hepatitis](https://www.rchsd.org/programs-services/neonatology/parent-education/breastfeeding-and-hepatitis/)
- e-lactancia (last update 10 June 2018): IgM and IgG antibodies and, occasionally, hepatitis A virus RNA have been detected in breast milk, but "there have been no cases of vertical mother-infant transmission through breastmilk"; recommends that all mothers with hepatitis A continue breastfeeding; if the maternal infection is recent (<15 days) the infant should be protected with vaccine or standard immunoglobulin. — [e-lactancia – Maternal hepatitis A infection](https://e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/maternal-hepatitis-a-infection/product/)
- MotherToBaby fact sheet: "most women who are nursing can continue to breastfeed while they have a hepatitis A infection"; the baby might need immune globulin shots for protection; careful handwashing stressed. — [MotherToBaby – Hepatitis A](https://mothertobaby.org/fact-sheets/hepatitis-a/)
- Post-exposure prophylaxis for an exposed infant: 2020 ACIP recommendations table lists **0.1 mL/kg body weight of immune globulin (IG) for healthy children aged 0–11 months, with no hepatitis A vaccine** (vaccine is licensed from 12 months). MMR should not be given for ≥2 weeks before and 6 months after IG. — [ACIP 2020 hepatitis A recommendations, Table 4 (PMC8344968)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8344968/table/T4)
- IG must be given within 2 weeks after exposure for maximum protection. — [CDC – Hepatitis A clinical care](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-care/); see also [CDC MMWR 2018;67(43) update on HAV PEP](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/67/wr/mm6743a5.htm)
- CDC hepatitis A page: "no observed increase in maternal or infant adverse events after hepatitis A vaccination or immune globulin administration in pregnancy." — [CDC – Hepatitis A clinical care](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-a/hcp/clinical-care/)

**Hepatitis E (HEV) — uncertain area**
- e-lactancia (last update 14 July 2023): "Although anti-HEV antibodies and HEV RNA have been found in colostrum and milk (Rivero 2016, Chibber 2004), transmission through breastfeeding has not been documented"; states hepatitis E does not contraindicate breastfeeding. — [e-lactancia – Hepatitis E](https://e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/hepatitis-e/product/)
- MSF Clinical Guidelines (page marked "Content updated: February 2026"): "Breastfeeding is recommended." — [MSF medical guidelines, node 2331](https://medicalguidelines.msf.org/en/node/2331) (search extract only; could not open page to confirm the sentence sits in the hepatitis E/viral hepatitis section)
- StatPearls (Hepatitis E): "While there is insufficient data regarding HEV transmission via breast milk, the virus has been isolated in breast milk." — [StatPearls – Hepatitis E, NBK532278](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532278/)
- Chibber et al. 2004 (Arch Gynecol Obstet): 93 HEV-infected women followed from third trimester to 9 months postpartum; all infants seronegative at birth; anti-HEV antibodies and HEV RNA were found in colostrum; authors concluded "breast-feeding appears to be safe for these infants. However this report must be confirmed by others"; further studies needed on transmission through close contact. — [Chibber et al., Arch Gynecol Obstet 2004](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00404-002-0466-5)
- Rivero-Juarez A, Frias M, Rodriguez-Cano D, Cuenca-López F, Rivero A. "Isolation of Hepatitis E Virus From Breast Milk During Acute Infection" (Clin Infect Dis 2016, letter) — the main basis for caution during acute maternal infection. — cited in [e-lactancia – Hepatitis E](https://e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/hepatitis-e/product/)
- A review article returned by the search states: "breastfeeding is considered safe in asymptomatic women infected with HEV despite the presence of anti-HEV antibodies and HEV RNA in the colostrum", but for symptomatic infection "feeding formula is advised, as there is a possibility of transmission from infected breast milk or lesions on the nipple through suckling" and "breastfeeding is not advisable for symptomatic infection". — review on PubMed Central; the search tool did not disambiguate which of two returned reviews carried the wording: [PMC4501603](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501603) or [PMC5035967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5035967). **Conflicts with** MSF and e-lactancia above.
- EASL 2018 Clinical Practice Guidelines on HEV (J Hepatol 2018;68(6):1256–1271): no breastfeeding statement was found in any extract. — [EASL CPG HEV 2018 PDF](https://easl.eu/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/EASL-CPG-hepatitis-E-virus-infection.pdf)

**Hepatitis B (HBV)**
- CDC: mothers with hepatitis B or C infections can breastfeed their infants, with some precautions. For infants born to HBV-infected mothers: HBIG and first vaccine dose within 12 hours of birth. — [CDC – Hepatitis B or C Infections and Breastfeeding](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/illnesses-conditions/hepatitis-b-c.html)
- LactMed: "no differences exist in infection rates between breastfed and formula-fed infants born to hepatitis B-infected women, as long as the infant receives these preventative measures at birth" (HBIG + vaccine). — [LactMed HBIG record (PubMed 29999867)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29999867/); mirror: [Drugs.com LactMed – HBIG](https://www.drugs.com/breastfeeding/hepatitis-b-immune-globulin-human.html)
- Peer-reviewed: "Breastfeeding Is Not a Risk Factor for Mother-to-Child Transmission of Hepatitis B Virus" (title of study). — [PMC3557270](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3557270/)

**Hepatitis C (HCV)**
- CDC: "there is no documented evidence that breastfeeding spreads HCV". — [CDC – Hepatitis B or C Infections and Breastfeeding](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/illnesses-conditions/hepatitis-b-c.html)
- CDC, cracked or bleeding nipples (applies to HBV and HCV): there is not enough data to conclude whether breastfeeding can continue; because both viruses spread through blood, the mother "should stop breastfeeding temporarily", can "express and discard breast milk until her nipples are healed" to maintain supply, and "once her nipples are no longer cracked or bleeding, she may fully resume breastfeeding". — [CDC – Hepatitis B or C Infections and Breastfeeding](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/illnesses-conditions/hepatitis-b-c.html)
- State-level variant (not CDC wording): with an open breast sore or cracked, bleeding nipple the mother may breastfeed from the unaffected side and express and discard milk from the affected side until healed. — [Oregon Health Authority, Babies First Guideline 4](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/PH/HEALTHYPEOPLEFAMILIES/BABIES/HEALTHSCREENING/BABIESFIRST/Pages/Guideline-4.aspx)

### Inferences
- In this household the mother is *exposed, not ill*. None of the sources found advises stopping breastfeeding for a well, exposed mother for any of the four viruses; the relevant actions are hygiene (HAV/HEV) and prophylaxis (HAV IG for the infant; HBV vaccine ± HBIG for the mother if the father has acute hepatitis B).
- For HEV the honest message is "continue breastfeeding while the mother is well; if she becomes jaundiced, the decision should be made with a doctor because expert sources disagree" — this is an inference from the conflict between MSF/e-lactancia and the review advising formula in symptomatic infection.
- Availability of intramuscular immune globulin for HAV prophylaxis in Bangladesh is unknown (see Gaps); the family should ask a paediatrician, ideally within 14 days of last exposure.

### Gaps
- **CDC's dedicated hepatitis A / breastfeeding wording and AAP Red Book wording could not be retrieved** (cdc.gov blocked; search extracts did not include them). The HAV statements above rest on a hospital handout, e-lactancia and MotherToBaby. Unverified recollection (do not cite without checking): the AAP Red Book and CDC both state maternal HAV is not a contraindication to breastfeeding.
- AAP policy statement on breastfeeding (2022) — not retrieved.
- No WHO document specifically on HEV and breastfeeding was found. No EASL statement on breastfeeding found.
- No data found on HEV viral load thresholds in milk or on any documented infant infection via milk.
- Whether IG (GamaSTAN-type product) is obtainable in Bangladesh — no source found.

## 2. Is it safe for the breastfeeding mother to receive hepatitis A vaccine, hepatitis B vaccine, immune globulin or HBIG?

### Takeaway
Yes for both vaccines per LactMed/CDC-derived sources — breastfeeding is not a contraindication. HBIG/IG are antibody preparations; LactMed found little direct data, e-lactancia rates HBIG compatible, and one drug-label-based source says "caution".

### Cited Findings
- LactMed, hepatitis A vaccine (record last revised 15 June 2024): breastfeeding is not a contraindication to the vaccine; "The CDC and several health professional organizations state that vaccines given to a nursing mother do not affect the safety of breastfeeding for mothers or infants"; limited data indicate breastfeeding can enhance the infant's response to certain vaccine antigens; no published information found on vaccine components in milk. — [LactMed – Hepatitis A Vaccine, NBK500932](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK500932)
- Immunize.org: lactating women can receive HepA vaccine because it "is an inactivated vaccine and poses no harm to the nursing infant". — [Immunize.org Ask the Experts](https://www.immunize.org/ask-experts/can-lactating-women-receive-hepa-vaccine)
- MotherToBaby: CDC does not consider hepatitis A vaccine a reason to stop breastfeeding; breastfeeding does not affect how well the vaccine works. — [MotherToBaby – Hepatitis A vaccine](https://mothertobaby.org/fact-sheets/hepatitis-a-vaccine/)
- LactMed, hepatitis B vaccine: "breastfeeding is not a contraindication to the vaccine"; "Breastfeeding improves the infant's response to hepatitis B immunization". — [Drugs.com LactMed mirror – Hepatitis B vaccine](https://drugs.com/breastfeeding/hepatitis-b-vaccine.html)
- LactMed, HBIG: describes HBIG as an antibody-rich immune globulin; reports only maternal IgG/IgM levels in colostrum/milk of a few mothers; no adverse infant effect statement located in extract. — [LactMed HBIG (PubMed 29999867)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29999867/)
- e-lactancia (updated 4 Jan 2022): HBIG compatible with breastfeeding — "very high molecular weight" makes passage of significant quantities into milk highly unlikely. — [e-lactancia – Hepatitis B immunoglobulin](https://www.e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/hepatitis-b-immunoglobulin/product/)
- Contrasting label-derived wording: "caution is recommended", milk excretion unknown. — [Drugs.com – HBIG pregnancy and breastfeeding warnings](https://drugs.com/pregnancy/hepatitis-b-immune-globulin.html)
- Prophylaxis for the wife if the husband has **acute hepatitis B**: CDC — susceptible persons exposed through sexual contact with a person with acute or chronic HBV "should receive postexposure prophylaxis with 0.06 mL/kg of HBIG in a single IM dose within 14 days of their last exposure; early administration may be more effective", followed by the standard three-dose vaccine series; single-dose HBIG ≈75% effective if given within 2 weeks of last sexual exposure; HBIG + vaccine is the regimen of choice. — [CDC MMWR RR-5416 Appendix](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/rr5416a4.htm); [CDC STI Treatment Guidelines – HBV](https://www.cdc.gov/std/treatment-guidelines/hbv.htm)
- Timing differs by country: Ireland — ideally within 48 hours, not later than one week ([HPSC algorithm](https://www.hpsc.ie/a-z/hepatitis/hepatitisb/guidancepublications/AlgorithmAcuteHepBContacts.pdf)); England — contacts seen within one week of last contact offered HBIG + vaccine ([UKHSA HBIG guidance, March 2021](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/immunoglobulin-when-to-use/hepatitis-b-immunoglobulin-issued-march-2021)).
- Non-sexual household contacts of acute HBV: HBIG generally only if there was a blood exposure (e.g., shared razor or toothbrush), plus vaccine series. — [Iowa HHS Epi Manual – Hepatitis B](https://hhs.iowa.gov/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis-b-0)

### Inferences
- A breastfeeding wife can be vaccinated against hepatitis A and B without interrupting breastfeeding; HBIG after sexual exposure to acute HBV is also not a reason to stop.
- The infant in Bangladesh is likely already partly/fully vaccinated against HBV via pentavalent at 6, 10, 14 weeks (see section 9) — family should check the EPI card.

### Gaps
- LactMed's standalone "Immune Globulin" (non-specific IG) record wording was not retrieved.
- No hepatitis E vaccine guidance for breastfeeding women found (Hecolin is not WHO-prequalified/routinely available in Bangladesh as far as this search showed — unverified).

## 3. If the mother herself becomes jaundiced: what to do about breastfeeding

### Takeaway
Do not stop breastfeeding automatically: HAV — continue with handwashing and get IG for the infant; HBV/HCV — continue unless nipples are cracked/bleeding; HEV — continue per MSF/e-lactancia, but this is disputed for symptomatic acute HEV, so get same-day medical advice. A jaundiced pregnant or postpartum woman needs hospital assessment regardless.

### Cited Findings
- HAV: "even during the acute infectious period, is not a contraindication to breastfeeding". — [Rady Children's](https://www.rchsd.org/programs-services/neonatology/parent-education/breastfeeding-and-hepatitis/); continue and protect infant with IG/vaccine if maternal infection <15 days old. — [e-lactancia HAV](https://e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/maternal-hepatitis-a-infection/product/)
- HBV/HCV: can breastfeed; temporarily stop and express/discard if nipples cracked or bleeding, then fully resume. — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/breastfeeding-special-circumstances/hcp/illnesses-conditions/hepatitis-b-c.html)
- HEV: "Breastfeeding is recommended" — [MSF](https://medicalguidelines.msf.org/en/node/2331); not contraindicated — [e-lactancia HEV](https://e-lactancia.org/breastfeeding/hepatitis-e/product/); versus "breastfeeding is not advisable for symptomatic infection … feeding formula is advised" — review ([PMC4501603](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4501603) / [PMC5035967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5035967), attribution between the two unverified).
- HAV exposure window used for contact protection: 14 days before symptom onset through 7 days after onset. — [Iowa HHS Epi Manual – Hepatitis A](https://hhs.iowa.gov/public-health/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis/controlling)

### Inferences
- In Bangladesh, replacing breast milk with formula carries its own serious infection/diarrhoea risk (unsafe water, bottles), so advice to switch to formula for maternal HEV (from a review, not a guideline) must be weighed against that; the guideline-level sources found (MSF, updated Feb 2026) favour continuing.
- If the mother is too ill to feed, expressed breast milk fed by cup is a reasonable bridge — inference; no specific source found for the hepatitis context.

### Gaps
- No Bangladesh national guidance on breastfeeding during maternal acute viral hepatitis was found.
- No source found on safety of specific drugs the mother might be given while jaundiced and breastfeeding.

## 4. Infant feeding hygiene: 0–6 months (exclusive breastfeeding) and 6–12 months (complementary feeding)

### Takeaway
WHO: breast milk only for the first 6 months — "not even water"; from 6 months add safe complementary foods and keep breastfeeding to 2 years or beyond. Bangladesh's national IYCF strategy/communication plan makes handwashing before feeding a key behaviour, and icddr,b data show handwashing before food preparation is associated with markedly less child diarrhoea — yet it is rarely practised.

### Cited Findings
- WHO definition: "Exclusive breastfeeding means no other food or drink, not even water, except breastmilk (including milk expressed or from a wet nurse) for the first 6 months of life, with the exception of rehydration solution (ORS), drops and syrups (vitamins, minerals and medicines)." — [WHO EMRO – Exclusively breastfeed for 6 months](https://www.emro.who.int/nutrition/breastfeeding/exclusively-breastfeed-for-6-months.html)
- "From the age of 6 months, children should begin eating safe and adequate complementary foods while continuing to breastfeed for up to two years of age or beyond." — [WHO – Infant and young child feeding fact sheet](https://www.who.int/News-Room/Fact-Sheets/Detail/Infant-and-Young-Child-Feeding)
- WHO Guideline for complementary feeding of infants and young children 6–23 months (published 2023; supersedes the earlier Guiding Principles): introduce complementary foods at 6 months (180 days) while continuing to breastfeed; diverse diet; animal-source foods (meat, fish or eggs) daily; sweetened milks not appropriate. — [WHO 2023 guideline](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240081864)
- WHO complementary feeding page: 2–3 meals/day at 6–8 months, 3–4/day at 9–11 and 12–24 months; gradually increase consistency and variety; responsive feeding. — [WHO – Complementary feeding](https://www.who.int/health-topics/complementary-feeding)
- Bangladesh: National IYCF Strategy (2007) — goal to improve nutritional status, growth, development, health and survival through optimal IYCF practices; objectives include increasing the percentage of infants <6 months exclusively breastfed. — [National Strategy for IYCF in Bangladesh (Save the Children resource centre)](https://resourcecentre.savethechildren.net/document/national-strategy-for-infant-and-young-child-feeding-in-bangladesh)
- Bangladesh: National Communication Framework and Plan for IYCF (IPHN, DGHS, 2010) — handwashing before feeding treated as a key behaviour because "complementary foods are a major source of pathogens leading to diarrhoea"; counselling covers breastfeeding, complementary feeding and handwashing before feeding at specific ages. — [IPHN National IYCF Communication Plan (Alive & Thrive copy)](https://www.aliveandthrive.org/sites/default/files/attachments/Toolkit-R2A-National-IYCF-Communication-Plan.pdf); [SUN copy, 24-11-2010](https://scalingupnutrition.org/sites/default/files/2021-12/IYCF-Plan-Document-24-11-2010.pdf)
- icddr,b/Luby et al., PLoS Medicine 2011 (347 households, 50 villages, rural Bangladesh, 2007; observational): handwashing before food preparation — even with water alone — was associated with less child diarrhoea; authors: "handwashing before preparing food is a particularly important opportunity to prevent childhood diarrhea"; programmes should prioritise washing after defecation and before food preparation. The OR 0.45 relates to soap use after defecation; soap before feeding/eating or after cleaning a child's anus showed no significant association. — [Luby et al. 2011, PMC3125291](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3125291)
- Children whose caregivers washed at least one hand with soap during food preparation had significantly fewer days of diarrhoea (3.7%) than children whose caregivers did not wash hands at all during food preparation (12.5%). — reported in search extract as "one cited study", most consistent with [Luby et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3125291) (exact source attribution to be verified)
- An icddr,b observation study in three villages found only 1% of food preparers were observed washing hands with soap during food preparation. — one of the icddr,b-linked papers returned: [PMC4973173](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4973173) / [PMC5303048](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5303048) (attribution between the two unverified)
- Cost of bar soap is a barrier; icddr,b has tested lower-cost handwashing options. — [icddr,b news: "Could new icddr,b interventions finally achieve 'Clean Hands for All'?"](https://www.icddrb.org/news-and-events/news?id=866)
- Practice gap: in a 2014 national survey only 23% of caregivers of children 6–23 months maintained adequate IYCF practices; complementary feeding practices did not improve 2004–2014. — [PMC6680185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6680185) / [PMC7988846](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7988846) (attribution between the two unverified)

### Inferences
- With a faecal-orally transmitted virus (HAV/HEV) possibly in the household, exclusive breastfeeding is itself the main protection for a 0–6-month infant because it removes water and food as exposure routes.
- For 6–12 months: wash hands with soap after toilet/diaper change and before preparing food and before feeding; freshly cooked food served hot; boiled/safe water; clean cup, bowl and spoon instead of bottles — standard WHO/UNICEF counselling; explicit source wording for the cooked-food/boiled-water/no-bottle points was not retrieved (see Gaps).
- The jaundiced father should not prepare the infant's food while he may be infectious (see section 5).

### Gaps
- Exact WHO/UNICEF wording on "practise good hygiene and proper food handling", avoiding feeding bottles, and boiling water was **not retrieved** (who.int blocked; search extract truncated). The WHO IYCF fact sheet and the superseded PAHO/WHO *Guiding Principles for Complementary Feeding of the Breastfed Child* (2003) are where this wording is believed to sit — unverified.
- The current (post-2007) version of Bangladesh's national IYCF strategy/guideline, if any, was not found.
- No Bangladesh-specific evidence found on household water treatment (boiling) and HEV/HAV risk in infants.

## 5. Should the jaundiced father hold, kiss, feed or change diapers? Casual contact, premastication, shared spoons

### Takeaway
HBV and HCV are not spread by hugging, kissing, holding or sharing utensils (CDC) — but pre-chewing food for a baby is a documented HBV route and must be avoided. HAV and (less efficiently) HEV are faecal-oral: holding is fine, but the father should not prepare food or feed the baby during the infectious period and everyone must wash hands with soap after the toilet/diaper changes.

### Cited Findings
- HCV: "There is no evidence to suggest that hepatitis C is spread by sharing eating utensils, hugging, kissing, holding hands, coughing, or sneezing." — [CDC – Hepatitis C prevention and control](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-c/prevention)
- HBV: "Although the virus can be found in saliva, it is not spread through kissing or sharing utensils"; hugging and food/water also listed as non-routes. — [CDC – Hepatitis B prevention and control](https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/prevention/)
- Premastication: "transmission of hepatitis B virus and group A streptococcus by premastication has been documented" (CDC MMWR 2011, report mainly on HIV). — [CDC MMWR 60(09), 2011](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6009a2.htm)
- Premastication exposes the infant to agents in the caregiver's saliva and blood, especially with periodontal disease; includes HIV and hepatitis B. — [Medical Algorithms – hazards of premasticating food for an infant](https://www.medicalalgorithms.com/hazards-of-premasticating-food-for-an-infant) (secondary/low-authority source)
- HBV blood-exposure items in households: shared razors or toothbrushes count as blood exposure. — [Iowa HHS Epi Manual – Hepatitis B](https://hhs.iowa.gov/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis-b-0)
- HAV infectious period: peak infectivity from 2 weeks before jaundice (or ALT elevation) through 1 week after jaundice onset. — [California DPH Hepatitis A Quicksheet](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CID/DCDC/CDPH%20Document%20Library/Immunization/HepatitisA-Quicksheet.pdf); 14 days before to 7 days after symptom onset — [Iowa HHS](https://hhs.iowa.gov/public-health/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis/controlling)
- HAV food handling: infected persons should not handle food for others for 1 week after jaundice onset ([Texas DSHS 2023](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/sites/default/files/IDCU/investigation/electronic/EAIDG/2023/Hepatitis-A.pdf); California Quicksheet above); stricter: until 7 days after jaundice or 2 weeks after symptoms began (Iowa, above); 1–2 weeks after jaundice ([Alaska](https://health.alaska.gov/media/nfupfwqz/mmm_chapter_hepatitisa.pdf)). Jurisdictions differ.
- HAV hygiene: thorough handwashing after defecation and diaper changing and before handling food ([Oregon Health Authority HAV guideline](https://www.oregon.gov:443/oha/PH/DISEASESCONDITIONS/COMMUNICABLEDISEASE/REPORTINGCOMMUNICABLEDISEASE/REPORTINGGUIDELINES/Documents/hepa.pdf)); dispose of or thoroughly wash nappies of infected infants ([Victoria Dept of Health – Hepatitis A](https://health.vic.gov.au/infectious-diseases/hepatitis-a)).
- Infected infants and children can shed HAV in stool for much longer than adults — up to 6 months reported. — state HAV guidance returned in search (e.g. [Texas DSHS 2022](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/sites/default/files/IDCU/investigation/electronic/EAIDG/2022/Hepatitis-A.pdf); exact source of the 6-month figure among the state documents unverified)
- HAV household contacts: post-exposure prophylaxis (IG or vaccine by age) as soon as possible and within 14 days of last exposure to an infectious case. — [Iowa HHS](https://hhs.iowa.gov/public-health/center-acute-disease-epidemiology/epi-manual/reportable-diseases/hepatitis/controlling)
- HEV person-to-person: "coincidental infections within households do occur, however person-to-person transmissions are rare"; incubation 15–60 days (average 40). — [UK Public Health Operational Guidelines for Hepatitis E](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5dc2d6a2ed915d394be4986c/Public_Health_Operational_Guidelines_for_Hepatitis_E-protection_response.pdf); "infrequent"; infectious period may extend beyond 6 weeks in immunosuppressed people — [Queensland Health – Hepatitis E](https://www.health.qld.gov.au/cdcg/index/hepe)
- Counter-evidence for HEV: large Northern Uganda outbreak — high household attack rate, no common source and poor household hygiene "suggest person-to-person transmission"; 25% of cases occurred ≥8 weeks after onset in a household index case. — [Teshale et al., Clin Infect Dis 2010 (PubMed 20178415)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20178415/)
- HEV shedding: a modelling paper cites WHO (2001) for faecal viral shedding lasting on average ~5 weeks. — [Mercer et al. 2011 (ANU repository)](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/bitstream/1885/25187/2/01_Mercer_Application_of_a_hepatitis_E_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because the cause is not yet known, the household should apply the stricter faecal-oral precautions (HAV/HEV) *and* the blood precautions (HBV/HCV) together until tests come back: father may hold/cuddle the baby with clean hands; should not prepare food, feed by hand, pre-chew food, taste-then-feed from the same spoon, or share razor/toothbrush/nail cutter; diaper changing best done by someone else, with soap handwashing afterwards. The "avoid shared spoon/taste-then-feed" point for HAV/HEV is an inference from faecal-oral/hand contamination logic and from the HBV premastication evidence; no guideline sentence specifically on shared spoons for HAV/HEV was found.
- By the time an adult with HAV is visibly jaundiced, peak infectivity (the 2 weeks before jaundice) has largely passed — so exposure of wife and infant has probably already occurred, which is why prophylaxis timing (≤14 days) matters more than isolation.
- Kissing on the mouth and sharing saliva is prudent to avoid while the cause is unknown, even though CDC says HBV/HCV are not spread by kissing — inference.

### Gaps
- No guideline found that directly addresses a jaundiced parent holding/kissing an infant.
- No South Asia-specific data on premastication practice and hepatitis transmission found.
- Duration of HEV stool shedding in immunocompetent adults rests on a modelling paper citing WHO 2001; no primary clinical study retrieved.

## 6. Warning signs in the infant (<12 months) needing urgent medical evaluation

### Takeaway
WHO IMCI general danger signs (cannot drink/breastfeed, vomits everything, convulsions, lethargic/unconscious) mean urgent referral. Jaundice in an infant older than 2 weeks is not "normal" and needs a bilirubin test; pale stools or dark urine are red flags for liver/bile disease.

### Cited Findings
- IMCI general danger signs (age 2 months–5 years): child is not able to drink or breastfeed; vomits everything; has had convulsions; is lethargic or unconscious; (is convulsing now). Any one sign = "very severe disease", urgent action/referral. — [WHO IMCI Chart Booklet, March 2014](https://apo.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241506823); wording seen via national adaptation [Pakistan IMNCI chart booklet 2019](https://sites.pitt.edu/~super1/Pirzado/Chartbooklet%202019%20Pakistan.pdf)
- Infants <2 months are assessed under a separate chart (WHO 2019 sick young infant chart booklet supersedes that section of the 2014 booklet). — [WHO – Management of the sick young infant aged up to 2 months: chart booklet](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241516365)
- NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN 2017 guideline: "any formula-fed infant noted to be jaundiced after 2 weeks of age should be evaluated for cholestasis with measurement of total and conjugated (direct) serum bilirubin. Conjugated (direct) hyperbilirubinemia (>1.0 mg/dL, 17 μmol/L) is considered pathological and warrants diagnostic evaluation"; breast-fed infants who appear otherwise well may be followed clinically until 3 weeks, then tested if still icteric; dark urine listed as a history finding of conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia. — [NASPGHAN/ESPGHAN 2017 cholestatic jaundice guideline (PDF)](https://papers.ucalgary.ca/paediatrics/assets/neonatal-cholestasis-naspghan-2017.pdf)
- Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne: fractionated bilirubin should be done in prolonged jaundice, unwell infants, suspected pathological jaundice, **pale stools or dark urine**; consult paediatric team if conjugated bilirubin >20% of total. — [RCH Clinical Practice Guideline – Jaundice in early infancy](https://www.rch.org.au/clinicalguide/guideline_index/jaundice_in_early_infancy/)
- CHOP: referral to a paediatric gastroenterologist is indicated if conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia is noted beyond 2 weeks of life. — [CHOP – Neonatal jaundice: when to refer](https://www.chop.edu/news/neonatal-jaundice-when-refer)

### Inferences
- For this family: take the infant to a doctor the same day for any of — yellow eyes/skin, dark (tea-coloured) urine, pale/clay/white stools, refusing feeds or unable to breastfeed, repeated vomiting ("vomits everything"), unusual sleepiness/hard to wake, convulsions, fever (any fever in an infant <3 months), signs of dehydration (few wet nappies, sunken eyes/fontanelle, dry mouth, no tears), bleeding or easy bruising. This combined list is an inference assembling IMCI danger signs and the cholestasis red flags; dehydration and bleeding items are standard paediatric teaching but were not individually sourced in this session.
- "Any jaundice beyond the newborn period is abnormal": supported for >2–3 weeks by NASPGHAN/RCH; for an older infant (e.g., 6–11 months) new jaundice is by extension always abnormal — inference.
- Young children with HAV are often asymptomatic or anicteric, so absence of jaundice in the infant does not exclude infection — widely stated but not sourced in this session (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- Exact IMCI dehydration sign wording ("sunken eyes", "skin pinch goes back slowly", "restless, irritable") not retrieved in this session.
- No source retrieved on clinical presentation of HAV/HEV in infants (proportion asymptomatic). Unverified recollection: CDC states ~70% of HAV infections in children <6 years are asymptomatic.
- No Bangladesh-specific infant jaundice referral guideline found.

## 7. Warning signs of acute liver failure in the adult patient, and jaundice in a pregnant woman — when to go to hospital

### Takeaway
Jaundice plus new confusion, excessive sleepiness, behaviour change, bleeding/bruising, vomiting blood or abdominal swelling = medical emergency (acute liver failure can develop within days). In Bangladesh, a pregnant woman with jaundice should go to hospital immediately: hepatitis E in pregnancy is a major cause of maternal death.

### Cited Findings
- Acute liver failure (ALF) is "a medical emergency that requires hospitalization"; can happen "in as little as 48 hours"; go to the nearest emergency room for sudden jaundice, upper-right abdominal pain, confusion, personality or behaviour changes, or vomiting blood. — [Mayo Clinic – Acute liver failure](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/acute-liver-failure/symptoms-causes/syc-20352863)
- Progression signs: confusion, extreme tiredness/sleepiness, mood changes, easy bruising or bleeding, abdominal fluid, vomiting blood, dark stools; mental changes "can begin as mild confusion but progress to coma, known as hepatic encephalopathy"; brain swelling can cause disorientation and seizures. — patient-information sources returned together: [Johns Hopkins Medicine – Acute liver failure](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/acute-liver-failure), [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/acute-liver-failure/symptoms-causes/syc-20352863), [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/acute-liver-failure) (sentence-level attribution among these not verified)
- Definition: hepatic encephalopathy plus INR ≥1.5 within 26 weeks of symptom onset in a person without prior chronic liver disease. — [StatPearls – Acute liver failure, NBK482374](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482374/); see also [MSD Manual Professional – Acute liver failure](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/hepatic-and-biliary-disorders/approach-to-the-patient-with-liver-disease/acute-liver-failure)
- WHO: acute hepatitis E is usually self-limiting but "in rare cases acute hepatitis E can be severe and result in fulminant hepatitis (acute liver failure)"; such patients are at risk of death; disease is typically most severe in the third trimester of pregnancy. — [WHO – Hepatitis E fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-e)
- Bangladesh: icddr,b/JHU analysis of rural surveillance 2001–2007 — acute hepatitis, most likely hepatitis E, responsible for ≈9.8% of pregnancy-associated deaths. — [Labrique et al., Emerg Infect Dis 2012;18(9)](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/18/9/12-0241)
- Bangladesh: verbal-autopsy analysis — 19–25% of maternal deaths were associated with jaundice; 58% of jaundice-related deaths in pregnant women have been linked to HEV in published literature. — [Gurley et al. (PMC3519295)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3519295); [ReliefWeb/icddr,b: "Hepatitis E – a preventable cause of one in five maternal deaths in Bangladesh"](https://reliefweb.int/report/bangladesh/hepatitis-e-preventable-cause-one-five-maternal-deaths-bangladesh)
- "As many as 30 percent of pregnant women who contract hepatitis E die from the infection." — [Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, 2016](https://publichealth.jhu.edu/2016/anemic-underweight-pregnant-women-at-greater-risk-for-deadly-hepatitis-e-study-suggests)
- A Bangladesh-focused review reported mortality of 58% among pregnant women with HEV-related acute liver failure. — returned in search (one of [PMC3437697](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3437697) / [PMC4710431](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4710431) / [PMC3940148](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3940148); exact attribution unverified)
- 2008–2009 urban HEV outbreak near Dhaka: increased maternal and neonatal mortality in the affected community; began with deaths of four pregnant women with jaundice. — [Gurley et al., Clin Infect Dis 2014;59(5):658](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/5/658/2895602)

### Inferences
- Practical hospital triggers for the father: drowsiness/confusion/day-night sleep reversal, any bleeding (gums, nose, black stool, vomiting blood), vomiting that prevents drinking, swelling of abdomen/legs, passing very little urine, worsening jaundice or fever persisting. "Persistent vomiting" and "reduced urine" are standard clinical triggers but were not individually sourced here.
- If the wife is or may be pregnant, this changes the urgency substantially: she needs antenatal/medical review now, safe water, and immediate hospital care at first sign of jaundice.

### Gaps
- WHO fact-sheet wording on hospitalisation ("hospitalization is required for people with fulminant hepatitis and should also be considered for symptomatic pregnant women") and the "20–25% third-trimester case fatality" figure were **not confirmed** in search extracts — unverified recollection; check the WHO fact sheet directly.
- No Bangladesh clinical referral protocol for acute viral hepatitis found.

## 8. Harmful practices to avoid during jaundice in Bangladesh/South Asia

### Takeaway
Most Bangladeshi patients with jaundice turn first to kobiraj/folk remedies; herbal and Ayurvedic products are a documented cause of drug-induced liver injury and death in South Asia. Paracetamol use was associated with death in a Bangladeshi HEV outbreak. Evidence for specific dietary taboos is weak/absent; clinicians advise a normal diet.

### Cited Findings
- Bangladesh mixed-methods study (Dec 2023–Apr 2024): 62% of jaundiced participants favoured traditional medicine; lack of information about conventional treatment strongly associated (OR 13.82, 95% CI 4.62–41.33); included interviews with five traditional healers. — ["Push and pull factors influencing preference for traditional healing by jaundiced patients in Bangladesh" (PMC11530012)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11530012/)
- Rural Bangladesh (Hossain et al. 2013; 100 respondents with reported "jaundeesh", 25 kabirajs): care sought mostly from folk and spiritual providers; only 19% went to allopathic providers; a large urban hepatitis E outbreak "nearly went undetected by the public health sector as most of the cases did not seek formal care". Kabiraj described as a provider combining ayurvedic medicine and faith healing. — [Hossain et al., J Health Popul Nutr 2013 (PMC3805887)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3805887/)
- India: large single-centre series — Ayurvedic medicines caused drug-induced liver injury in 1.3% of patients and almost half of those affected died of progressive liver failure; toxicity attributed to direct herb effects, metabolites, herb–herb and herb–drug interactions, adulteration with prescription medicines, and poor manufacturing. — [Case series and review of Ayurvedic medication induced liver injury (PMC7956115)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7956115/); related review [World J Hepatol 2020;12(9):574](https://dx.doi.org/10.4254/wjh.v12.i9.574)
- North-East India: unknown herbal medications were a significant cause of mortality among patients with acute liver failure. — returned in search (one of [PMC5680208](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5680208) / [PMC7522561](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7522561) / [PMC6783675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6783675) / [PMC7438347](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7438347); exact attribution unverified)
- Case reports: liver injury from an indigenous herbal drug, *Tinospora cordifolia* (guduchi/giloy) — [Bengal Physician Journal, Mar 2025](https://doaj.org/article/347c4cb6a5824513bec574c9cff2d00e); ashwagandha-induced acute liver injury — [PubMed 36937644](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36937644/), [PubMed 34882134](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34882134/)
- Paracetamol: in the 2008–2009 urban Bangladesh HEV outbreak, "case-patients who took paracetamol during their illness were more likely to die than other case-patients." — [Gurley et al., Clin Infect Dis 2014](https://academic.oup.com/cid/article/59/5/658/2895602)
- Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose is the most common cause of acute liver failure in many settings. — [Mayo Clinic – Acute liver failure](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/acute-liver-failure/symptoms-causes/syc-20352863)
- Diet myths (low-authority sources; physician-quoted news/hospital blogs, not guidelines): jaundiced people should keep a normal diet unless an underlying disease requires restriction — [myRepublica (Nepal)](https://myrepublica.nagariknetwork.com/index.php/news/jaundiced-beware-of-some-myths); eating yellow foods (dal, turmeric, lemon) does not cause jaundice and turmeric's colour does not raise bilirubin — [LatestLY 2020](https://www.latestly.com/lifestyle/health-wellness/world-hepatitis-day-2020-can-hepatitis-really-cause-jaundice-most-common-myths-about-the-liver-disease-that-needs-to-be-busted-right-now-1920462.html), [Complete Wellbeing](https://completewellbeing.com/?p=2540); lean protein as part of a balanced diet — [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/food-nutrition/diet-for-jaundice). Conflicting: one Indian hospital blog advises avoiding chicken, mutton and seafood — [Manipal Hospitals](https://www.manipalhospitals.com/gurugram/blog/jaundice-diet-what-to-eat-and-avoid-for-faster-recovery/) — not supported by the other sources.
- Jaundice outbreaks in Bangladesh are linked to unsafe food and water: Rajshahi Jan–Mar 2024 outbreaks pointed to hepatitis A; study calls for safe food and drinking water. — [Discover Public Health 2025 study summary (Springer Nature community post)](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/study-of-an-outbreak-of-jaundice-in-rajshahi-bangladesh-a-sociodemographic-and-clinical-manifestation)

### Inferences
- Advise: no kobiraji/herbal/"jaundice mala"/unlabelled tonics; no alcohol; paracetamol only if a doctor says so and never above the advised dose (the Bangladesh outbreak association is observational, not proof of causation, but supports caution); no unprescribed medicines.
- Street-sold sugarcane juice, sherbet and cut fruit are plausible HAV/HEV sources because they use untreated water/ice — inference from faecal-oral transmission; **no source directly linking sugarcane juice to hepatitis in Bangladesh was found**.
- Severe food restriction (no oil, no protein, only boiled vegetables/glucose water) risks under-nutrition in the patient and, if imposed on a breastfeeding mother, has no hepatitis-prevention benefit — inference.

### Gaps
- No Bangladesh-specific study quantifying herbal/kobiraji-induced liver injury was found (only care-seeking studies and Indian DILI series).
- No guideline-level (WHO/EASL/AASLD) statement on diet in acute viral hepatitis was retrieved; diet evidence here is weak.
- Alcohol-avoidance wording from WHO/CDC not retrieved (standard advice; unverified in this session).
- No source on sugarcane juice contamination.

## 9. Supportive care facts for acute viral hepatitis in adults; relevant Bangladesh vaccination context

### Takeaway
There is no specific antiviral for acute hepatitis A or E; care is supportive (rest, fluids, nutrition) and recovery can take weeks to months. Almost all HAV patients recover fully with lifelong immunity.

### Cited Findings
- WHO: "There is no specific antiviral treatment for hepatitis A." — [WHO – Hepatitis A fact sheet](https://who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-a)
- "Management focuses on supportive care to relieve symptoms and ensure adequate hydration and nutrition"; "recovery from symptoms may be slow and can take several weeks or months"; almost everyone recovers fully with lifelong immunity; a very small proportion die from fulminant hepatitis. — [WHO Viet Nam – Hepatitis A fact sheet](https://www.who.int/vietnam/news/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-a); [WHO – Hepatitis A fact sheet](https://who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-a)
- WHO: "There is no specific antiviral treatment capable of altering the course of acute hepatitis E"; usually acute and self-limiting. — [WHO – Hepatitis E fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/hepatitis-e)
- HEV incubation 15–60 days (average 40 days). — [UK Public Health Operational Guidelines for Hepatitis E](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5dc2d6a2ed915d394be4986c/Public_Health_Operational_Guidelines_for_Hepatitis_E-protection_response.pdf)
- Bangladesh EPI: pentavalent vaccine (diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B, Hib) with OPV and PCV at 6, 10 and 14 weeks; hepatitis B vaccine introduced 2003–2005; HepB3 coverage 94% in 2015. — [Jagonews24 – Child immunisation: when and which to give](https://www.jagonews24.com/en/national/news/91363); [icddr,b – EPI vaccinations](https://www.icddrb.org/services/epi-vaccinations); [WHO Bangladesh EPI factsheet 2024](https://www.who.int/bangladesh/about-us/publications/i/item/bangladesh-epi-factsheet-2024)
- Bangladesh has still not introduced a hepatitis B **birth dose**; experts were urging its introduction as of July 2026. — [bdnews24, July 2026](https://bdnews24.com/health/28de6952476f)

### Inferences
- An infant who has completed three pentavalent doses (by ~14 weeks) should have good HBV protection; an infant younger than 6 weeks, or with missed doses, is unprotected against HBV — if the father proves to have hepatitis B, the paediatrician should be asked about early/accelerated vaccination.
- Hepatitis A vaccine appears not to be in the national EPI (not listed in any EPI source found) and is not usable under 12 months anyway — see Gaps.

### Gaps
- WHO wording on avoiding unnecessary medication ("acetaminophen/paracetamol and medication against vomiting should be avoided" — unverified recollection of the WHO hepatitis A fact sheet) was not found in the extracts.
- Typical duration figures (e.g., HAV symptoms usually <2 months; 10–15% relapse up to 6 months per CDC) not retrieved — unverified recollection.
- Explicit confirmation that hepatitis A vaccine is absent from Bangladesh EPI not found.
- EASL/AASLD supportive-care wording not retrieved.

## 10. Where families in Bangladesh can get help

### Takeaway
The national health call centre Shastho Batayon 16263 was still being reported as operating 24/7 in Oct–Nov 2026, though a 2026 Daily Star report says it is under strain; the call is not toll-free. Two Dhaka institutions have been renamed: BSMMU is now Bangladesh Medical University (BMU) and Sheikh Russel National Gastroliver Institute is now National Gastroliver Institute & Hospital.

### Cited Findings
- Shastho Batayon 16263: 24/7 government health call centre for medical consultation and advice, run under MIS, DGHS, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare; IT support by Synesis IT; also SMS 01511316263, email 16263@mis.dghs.gov.bd; doctors consult by phone and send e-prescriptions by SMS. — [NTV Online – Govt opens call centre for health services](https://en.ntvbd.com/node/735201)
- Launched September 2015; not toll-free; managed by Synesis IT Ltd. — [Prothom Alo – স্বাস্থ্য বাতায়ন ১৬২৬৩](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/স্বাস্থ্য-বাতায়ন-১৬২৬৩-উন্নয়নের-লক্ষ্যে-জনগণের)
- COVID-era scale: about 200 physicians per day; also ambulance information, health information and complaints about public/private medical services; 10 million advised during the pandemic. — [The Business Standard](https://www.tbsnews.net/node/151003)
- **Currency check (2026):** Jagonews24 report dated 11 Nov 2026 lists 16263 as the 24/7 call-centre number; a 13 Oct 2026 Jagonews24 report says dialling 16263 can connect an ambulance. — [Jagonews24, 11 Nov 2026](https://www.jagonews24.com/health/news/962649)
